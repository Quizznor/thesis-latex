\chapter{Cosmic rays and extensive air showers}
\label{chap:cosmic-rays}

\CRs are particles of extraterrestrial origin that travel very close to the 
speed of light. Their relativistic kinetic energy $pc$ far surpasses their rest
mass $mc^2$. In particular, \UHECRs are typically defined as \CRs with energies
exceeding $\SI{1}{\exa\eV} = \SI{e18}{\eV}$ 
\cite{alvesbatistaOpenQuestionsCR2019} These microscopic fragments originate 
from some of the most dynamic processes in the universe. As such, they are of 
great interest to researchers. Not only do they hold the key to understanding 
particle interactions at extremely high energies and small scales, but can also
improve the understanding of the cosmos over the largest distances.

\section{Cosmic Ray Phenomenology}
\label{sec:cr-phenomenology}

\subsection{Historical Discovery}
\label{ssec:cr-history}

The study of cosmic rays is an active area of research and a field more than 
one hundred years in the making. The turn of the twentieth century saw many 
physicists testing the predictions of the newly developed theory of 
electromagnetism. Of special importance for this story are the advancements of 
Theodor Wulf, who, in 1909, developed an electrometer that is able to measure 
the ionization rate of the atmosphere. His invention was used to show the 
difference in air ionization rates on the ground, at the top of the Eiffel 
Tower \cite{wulfBeobachtungenUberStrahlung1910} and underwater
\cite{paciniRadiazionePenetranteAlla1912}. In 1912 Victor Hess continued this
study by examining spontaneous ionization during ballon flights above Germany 
and Austria \cite{hessElektrischeLeitfaehigkeitAtmosphaere1926}. With the 
ionization rate increasing the higher the balloon flew, Hess concluded that 
energetic particles are present in the upper atmosphere. His intuition proved 
correct and would eventually be honored with a Nobel prize in 1936 
\cite{nobelprizeoutreach2025NobelPrizePhysicsa}.

Given the context of research at the time, these energetic particles were
thought to be \EM radiation. Their extraterrestrial origin was demonstrated by 
Hess himself, as well as Werner Kolhörster by 1914 
\cite{kolhorsterMeasurementsPenetratingRadiation1913}. Consequentially, the 
term \textit{cosmic rays} was coined for the phenomenon. In 1929 Walter Bothe 
and Kolhörster attempted to investigate the nature of cosmic rays further by 
studying their interactions with heavy absorbers 
\cite{botheNaturHohenstrahlung1929}. They were surprised to find out that the 
particles they investigated had a penetrating power uncharacteristic for 
$\upgamma$-radiation. They concluded that \CRs are made up of, at least 
partially, corpuscular radiation, rather than photons.

Since then, the study of \CRs has lead to the discovery of the positron 
(Anderson 1933, Ref.\ \cite{andersonPositiveElectron1933}), the muon (Anderson,
Neddermeyer 1936, Ref.\ \cite{andersonElementaryParticlesMatter1949}), and a 
plethora of other particles. Today we know that \CRs are very diverse in 
nature. Their composition ranges from elementary particles like electrons, over
exotic mesons, to heavy nuclei. Photons too have been detected in \CR 
experiments, proving that the original name is not a complete misnomer.

An important milestone was further achieved by Pierre Auger in 1938. He 
conducted coincidence measurements in the swiss Alps and found that the rate of
coincidences between several \CR detectors is directly proportional to their 
separation \cite{augerExtensiveCosmicRayShowers1939}. This marks the discovery 
of \EAS, and the beginning of ground based \CR research in array detectors.

\subsection{Cosmic Ray Observables}

The many discoveries of new fragments in the cosmic radiation has resulted in
the creation of entirely new theories that aim to describe the diverse particle
zoo at hand. In general though, only a handful parameters, called hereafter \CR
observables, are sufficient to characterize the kinematics of a cosmic ray from
genesis at the source, over propagation, to detection on Earth. It follows a 
brief introduction to the most important properties.

\subsubsection{Energy}
\label{sssec:energy}

The energy of the \CR particle is perhaps its' most defining observable. After 
a particle is ejected from a \CR acceleration mechanism (\todo{ref}) it begins
a journey mostly consisting of adiabatic processes. This means the \CR energy 
dictates how (and how far) the particle propagates through spacetime, as well 
as the size of the atmospheric particle cascade it kicks off on Earth.

\todo{ref} will reveal that the energy distribution, also called the energy 
spectrum, of most \CR accelerators is a power law $E^{-\gamma}$ with a single
spectral index $\gamma$ and eventual cutoff at a maximum attainable energy. The
energy spectrum measured on Earth is not, as shown in \cref{fig:cr-spectrum}. 
It shows several different kinks, corresponding to a hardening (softening) of 
the spectrum, where the average energy of a \CR increases (decreases). These 
kinks in general correspond to transition between different \CR populations, 
that each have a respective particle flux, injection and cutoff energy. An 
example of how these transitions result in kinks is shown in 
\cref{fig:cr-spectrum-explanation}. To date, several transition have been 
observed in the \CR spectrum observed on Earth:

\begin{itemize}
	\vspace{0.7cm}
	\item \textbf{The knee} refers to a softening of the spectrum around 
	\SI{4}{\peta\eV}. The spectral index changes from $\gamma\approx2.7$
	below the knee to $\gamma\approx3.1$ above the knee 
	\cite{blumerCosmicRaysKnee2009}. It has been shown that this coincides
	with a transition in the mean mass of \CR particles 
	\cite{lhaasocollaborationMeasurementsAllParticleEnergy2024}. The knee 
	is thus thought to mark the transition from light \CRs 
	($m_\mathrm{CR} \leq m_\mathrm{He}$) to heavier hadronic cosmic rays.

	\vspace{0.7cm}
	\item \textbf{The second knee} is a further softening from 
	$\gamma\approx3.1$ at an energy below roughly \SI{500}{\peta\eV} to 
	$\gamma\approx3.25$ above \cite{naganoEnergySpectrumPrimary1992, 
	lawrenceCosmicRayEnergy1991, birdEvidenceCorrelatedChanges1993, 
	abu-zayyadMeasurementCosmicRayEnergy2001}. Like the softening that 
	occurs at \SI{4}{\peta\eV}, this feature is assumed to be composition
	related. However, mass sensitive studies around the second knee to date
	have not been able to confirm this theory with sufficient certainty.

	\vspace{0.7cm}
	\item \textbf{The ankle} marks the transition from $\gamma\approx3.25$ 
	to $\gamma\approx2.5$ at an energy of roughly \SI{5}{\exa\eV} 
	\cite{fenuCosmicRayEnergy2023}. The Pierre Auger observatory has 
	reported on a dipole in the arrival directions of \CRs above 
	\SI{8}{\exa\eV} (Ref.\ \cref{fig:dipole} and 
	\cite{thepierreaugercollaborationObservationLargescaleAnisotropy2017}), 
	coincident with the hardening of the energy spectrum. Since the 
	anisotropy points away from the galactic center the ankle is projected
	to represent the transition from a predominantly galactic \CR 
	population to an extragalactic \CR population.

	\vspace{0.7cm}
	\item \textbf{The instep} is a softening of the \UHECR spectrum at 
	\SI{13}{\exa\eV} where $\gamma$ decreases from $\sim2.5$ to $\sim3.0$
	\cite{aabMeasurementCosmicrayEnergy2020}. This feature can be 
	reproduced in simulations when altering the \CR mass composition from 
	extragalactic light \CRs to extragalactic heavy \CRs 
	\cite{tsunesadaJointAnalysisEnergy2022}. Similar to the second knee 
	feature, experiments to date do not have a sufficiently good mass 
	sensitivity to confirm this theory.

	\vspace{0.7cm}
	\item \textbf{The suppression} marks the steep falloff at the tail-end 
	of the (measurable) \CR energy spectrum. At an energy above 
	$\sim\SI{50}{\exa\eV}$ the spectral index drastically changes to 
	$\gamma\approx5.4$ \cite{aabMeasurementCosmicrayEnergy2020}. Cosmic 
	rays exceeding this energy are incredibly sparse. It is theorized that
	no process exists in the (local) cosmos that can reliably accelerate 
	particles beyond this cutoff energy 
	\cite{tsunesadaJointAnalysisEnergy2022, 
		greisenEndCosmicRaySpectrum1966,
	kuzminCosmicrayInteractionsPhotons1968}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Mass}

An almost negligible fraction of the total energy is (for massive particles) 
given by the rest mass of the cosmic ray. Given the miniscule relative strength
of gravity,\footnote{Compared to the \EM-, strong or weak force.} mass terms 
can be safely ignored in \CR acceleration and propagation. However, for 
composite particles the energy per nucleon, $E/A$, heavily influences the 
development of particle cascades stemming from inelastic interactions of the 
\CR with a target material (Ref.\ \todo{ref}).

\cref{fig:cr-composition} shows the distribution of different \CR primaries 
with energies up to a few \unit{\giga\eV} and sets them into context with the 
elemental abundance in the solar system. Both distributions largely agree, with
an exception around the atomic number $Z=4$ (Beryllium) and $Z=23$ (Vanadium). 
They are not common elements in the solar system due to radioactive 
instability. Meanwhile, the \CR mass composition shows an excess of these 
nuclei, which can be explained by spallation of primary \CRs 
\cite{hillierGammaRayAstronomy1984}.

  \unit{\giga\eV} (red), which appear to be more level. The data stem from 
  \cite{hillierGammaRayAstronomy1984}.}
  \label{fig:cr-composition}

Moving to higher energies, a direct detection of \CRs (via e.g.\ sattelites or 
balloons) becomes unfeasible, and ground-based \CR observatories take over. 
Relying on indirect detection methods, these observatories do not have the same
mass sensitivity, and can typically only separate a light from a heavy 
component \cite{pereiramartinsSearchMassDependent2024} \todo{emilys thesis?}. 
\UHECR analysis is usually grouped into four different mass proxies due to this
inexactness. The chosen proxies are hydrogen ($Z=1$), helium ($Z=2$),
iron ($Z=26$), and one (or all) of carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen 
($Z\in[13,\,15]$).\footnote{The CNO-cycle leads to a roughly equal abundance of 
all three elements, see \cref{fig:cr-composition}.} The relative fraction of 
these proxies evolves over the \CR energy, as shown from measurements by the 
Pierre Auger Collaboration in \cref{fig:auger-mix}. Hadronic interaction models
suggest that light elements dominate the composition at low energies, whereas 
an increase in the mean logarithmic mass, $\langle\mathrm{ln}A\rangle$, is 
observed with an increase in energy 
\cite{mayotteMeasurementInterpretationUHECR2025}.

\subsubsection{Charge}

The electromagnetic charge of a particle defines how strongly it couples to an
external (electro-)magnetic field. As established before, the gravitational 
influence on a \CR particle can be safely ignored due to its' miniscule mass.

Consequentially, only \EM-fields and the corresponding coupling parameter, the
electric charge $q$, are of importance when considering the \CRs movement 
through the cosmos. In particular, the rigidity of a relativistic charged 
particle (given in units of Volts) is defined as the ratio between energy and 
charge of a particle:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rigidity}
R = \frac{E}{q}. 
\end{equation}

Physically, a high value of $R$ can represent different scenarios, a particle 
of lower charge, or higher energy, for example. Mathematically, these examples 
result in the same (smaller) Lorentz deflection of the cosmic ray. This is
because a particle with lower charge couples less strongly to the \EM-field. A 
\CR with higher energy retains a higher inertia, and is more resistant to 
acceleration. Vice versa, particles of lower rigidity are influenced more 
heavily by electromagnetic interactions, and move through galaxies in a more
turbulent manner (c.f.\ \cref{fig:galactic-propagation}). Fundamentally, 
particles of equal rigidity behave equally in \EM-fields.

\subsection{Implications for Sources}

The next chapter discusses possible accelerator mechanisms of \CRs. Not much is
known with certainty about their origins and sources. Nevertheless, a lot can 
be learned about the nature of \CR sources from examining the distribution of 
\CR observables measured on Earth. A list of key insights is given in the next 
paragraphs.

\subsubsection{Constraints from Energy Spectrum}

The \CR energy spectrum (\cref{fig:cr-spectrum}) is well described by a broken
power law. The fact that several sharp transitions between different spectral
indices $\gamma$ exists is remarkable. A distribution of \CR sources with 
vastly different energy spectra could easily wash out the observed features. 
The presence of these breaks implies that the sources must thus be similar in 
nature, possibly relying on the same acceleration mechanisms.

\subsubsection{Constraints from Arrival Directions}

The arrival direction of \CRs on Earth is not uniformly distributed. This is 
(at lower energies) owed to the geomagnetic field 
\cite{barberEastWestAsymmetryLatitude1949}. But even at higher energies large 
scale anisotropies exist. \cref{fig:dipole} shows the deviation of the observed
\CR flux above \SI{8}{\exa\eV} from the isotropic expectation. A top-hat filter 
with a width of $\SI{45}{\degree}$ is applied to the data as described in 
\cite{thepierreaugercollaborationObservationLargescaleAnisotropy2017}. A dipole
roughly perpendicular to the \acf{SGP} is clearly visible. If \CR sources are 
scattered throughout the Universe uniformly then this observation implies that 
the most energetic \CR accelerators lie outside of the milky way.

\subsubsection{Constraints from Mass Composition}

Observations of the shower depth (c.f.\ \todo{ref}) from the Pierre Auger 
observatory hint towards the most energetic cosmic rays being heavy, whereas 
light and heavy particles constitute the \CR population at intermediate 
energies. These measurements support the conclusions made in the previous 
paragraphs and paint a conclusive picture of the nature of \CR accelerators;

The cosmic ray spectrum is made up of a galactic and an extragalactic 
component. Both components in turn can be divided into a population of light 
and heavy \CRs. The sources, following a Peters cycle 
\cite{petersPrimaryCosmicRadiation1961}, accelerate the particles to a cutoff
energy $E_\mathrm{max}\propto Z$. Extragalactic sources can have a higher
cutoff energy than those within the milky way. This explains the mixed 
composition of \CRs at intermediate energies. The \CR spectrum at energies up 
to $\sim\SI{1}{\exa\eV}$ consists of the heavy galactic component and the light
extragalactic component. In contrast, cosmic rays at higher energies originate
from outside the milky way and tend towards heavier particles.

\subsection{General Principles}
\label{ssec:hillas-criterion}

It has been established on the previous pages that \CR sources rely on similar,
if not the same principles to accelerate particles up to extreme energies. The
physical mechanism powering these processes must be the \EM force, as it is the
only long-range interaction of sufficient strength. All \CR source models thus
require a strong electric or magnetic field with which particles can interact 
and gain energy. For magnetic fields in particular an important criterion 
applies. Because 
$W_\mathrm{L}=\vec{F}_\mathrm{L}\cdot\vec{r}\propto\vec{v}\times\vec{r} = 0$, 
the magnetic force acts purely adiabatically in the magnetostatic case. It 
follows that the acceleration of a test particle with charge $q$ can only occur 
if the source magnetic field lines are evolving over time. This is most easily 
realized by rotating magnetic fields (of strength $\vec{B}$ and extent 
$L_{\vec{B}}$, rotating at a velocity $\vec{v}$), fueled e.g.\ by a dynamo 
effect \cite{straussDynamoEffectFusion1985}. A heuristic argument can then be 
made about the maximum energy $E_\mathrm{max}$ that a given accelerator can 
transfer to the test particle:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hillas-criterion}
\frac{|\vec{B}|}{\unit{\upmu\gauss}}\,\frac{L_{\vec{B}}}{\unit{\parsec}} > 2\,\frac{E_\mathrm{max}}{\unit{\peta\eV}}\,\frac{e}{q}\,\frac{v}{c}   
\end{equation}

The reasoning for this, as laid out in 
\cite{hillasOriginUltraHighEnergyCosmic1984}, is simple; The test particle will
be accelerated while it remains in the magnetic field of the source. This leads
to a steady increase in the energy and rigidity of the particle. With an
increase in rigidity the Larmor radius $R_\mathrm{L} = E\,/\,(qc|\vec{B}|)$
grows. \cref{eq:hillas-criterion} roughly marks the energy at which the Larmor 
radius exceeds the size of the source magnetic field, and the test particle 
must necessarily escape.

This so-called Hillas criterion provides an easy way of grouping accelerators 
based on their size and field strength. It also reveals that \CR sources have
such similar spectra precisely because they are comparable in size and field 
strength. \cref{fig:hillas-plot} shows the classes of \CR accelerators that can
be distinguished in this way. Moving on, this section focuses on the two source
candidates that are thought to be the dominating contributors for galactic- and
extragalactic \CRs, as well as the indirect production of uncharged \CRs.

\subsection{Supernova Remnants}
\label{ssec:supernova-remnants}

\SNRs are, as the name implies, what remains of massive stars after they meet 
their violent end in a supernova. Whether a supernova is triggered by thermal 
runaway or a core collapse, the stellar explosion releases a tremendous amount 
of energy.

For thermonuclear supernovae a runaway fusion reaction is thought to increase
the stars' temperature thousandfold in the span of a few seconds 
\cite{khokhlovLightCurvesType1993}. This rapid increase in internal energy far
surpasses the gravitational binding energy. The luminosity of the system 
rapidly increases and the star disintegrates.

On the contrary, core-collapse supernovae have the vast majority
($>\SI{90}{\percent}$) of the energy escape immediately in the form of 
neutrinos \cite{odrzywolekProbingThermonuclearSupernova2011}. The burst of 
weakly interacting particles is so immense that the escape blows off the outer 
layers of the star in the process, leaving behind only the core of the 
progenitor star \cite{walchEnergyMomentumInput2015}.

In both cases a shockwave of plasma is formed that moves at a small fraction
of the speed of light into the surrounding \ISM. Its' internal conductivity is 
infinite, as the plasma potential is the same everywhere within the shock 
front. It follows that internal magnetic field lines must be comoving within 
the plasma. Such moving magnetic fields provide an excellent condition for the 
genesis of cosmic rays.

\subsubsection{Diffusive Shock Acceleration}

\DSA describes the process by which a particle can gain energy and eventually 
escape as a cosmic ray from interacting elastically with the magnetic fields 
inside the shock front. For a mathematical description consider the scenarios
shown in \cref{fig:fermi-acceleration}; A \SNR with a magnetic field $\vec{B}$,
expanding with some velocity $\beta = |\vec{v}|/c$ into which a particle 
enters. $\vec{B}$ nor $\beta$ are, in the general case, neither isotropic nor 
constant. 

$E_0 \approx p_0c$ and $E_1 \approx p_1c$ denote the energy of the particle 
before and after traversing the shock front.\footnote{$E_0$ is not the rest 
mass of the particle, but its (relativistic) energy upon entering the mag. 
field.} Furthermore, the flow direction of the shock front determines a 
reference system by which one can describe the trajectory of the particle. As 
such, $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$ mark the entrance and exit angles with the 
shock front respectively.

All above quantities are defined w.r.t.\ a suitable, cosmic, restframe. The 
initial energy of the particle as observed from the shock restframe becomes

\begin{equation}
E_0' = E_0\,\frac{\left( 1 + \beta\cos\theta_0 \right)}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}.
\end{equation}

Because the field lines are static in the shock front rest frame it is 
$E_0' = E_1'$. Transforming back to the unprimed system 
($E_1=E_1'\,\left(1+\beta\cos\theta_1'\right)\,/\,\sqrt{1-\beta^2}$),

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fractional-energy-change}
\qquad\xi = \frac{E_1 - E_0}{E_0} = \frac{1 
	+ \beta\left(\cos\theta_0 + \cos\theta_1'\right) 
	+ \beta^2 \cos\theta_0\,\cos\theta_1'}{1-\beta^2} - 1. 
\end{equation}

The trivial cases $\beta=0$, $\theta_0 = \theta_1'=\pi$ yield a fractional 
change in energy $\xi=0$ as expected. In the general case, one can average over
the incident and reflection angle as done in e.g.\ 
\cite{gaisserCosmicRaysParticle2016} to retrieve a mean enery change 
$\langle\xi\rangle$. When doing so, an edge case of the magnetic scattering off
the cloud becomes apparent;

For a sufficiently large \SNR the scattering occurs at a shock front that is
essentially plane. This changes the mean geometry, specifically

\begin{equation}
	\langle\cos\theta_0\rangle_\mathrm{cloud} = -\frac{\beta}{3}, 
	\,\,\mathrm{and}\qquad\qquad 
	\langle\cos\theta_1'\rangle_\mathrm{cloud} = 0,
\end{equation}

while

\begin{equation}
	\langle\cos\theta_0\rangle_\mathrm{plane} = -\frac{2}{3},
	\,\,\mathrm{and}\qquad\qquad 
	\langle\cos\theta_1'\rangle_\mathrm{plane} = \frac{2}{3}.
\end{equation}

The mean fractional change in energy for the two scenarios becomes thus

\begin{equation}
	\langle\xi\rangle_\mathrm{cloud} \sim \frac{4}{3}\,\beta^2, 
	\qquad 
	\langle\xi\rangle_\mathrm{plane} \sim \frac{4}{3}\,\beta.
\end{equation}

Since $0\leq\beta<1$, the mean fractional change in energy is strictly positive
in either case. This implies that on average the particle gains energy from the
interactions. Moreover, it gains more energy from the interaction with the 
plane front. Considering the geometry in \cref{fig:fermi-type-i}, the process 
must be a (isotropic) reflection, with $\cos\theta_0<0$ and $\cos\theta_1'>0$. 
As such, the linear term in $\beta$ remains, unlike for the cloud scattering, 
for which the leading term is only quadratic in $\beta$.

The two processes are known as type I (plane) and type II (cloud) Fermi
acceleration, after the discovery of the effect by Enrico Fermi in 1949
\cite{fermiOriginCosmicRadiation1949}.

One interaction with the shock front can roughly double the energy of the 
incident particle, but not more. In order to increase its energy substantially,
the particle must cross the magnetic fields many times. After $n$ crossings the
energy of the particle reads on average 

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:energy-per-crossing}
\langle E_n \rangle = E_0\left( 1 + \langle\xi\rangle \right)^n. 
\end{equation}

However, with each crossing there also exists a probability $P_\mathrm{esc.}$, 
corresponding to the particle escaping instead of entering the accelerator for 
an $n+1$th crossing. The number of particles above a given energy thus behaves 
like 

\begin{equation}
	\langle N(\geq E_n)\rangle \sim \sum\limits_{m=n}^\infty 
	\left( 1 + P_\mathrm{esc.}\right)^m 
	= \frac{\left( 1-P_\mathrm{esc.} \right)^n}{P_\mathrm{esc.}}
	\propto \left(\frac{E}{E_0}\right)^{-\gamma}.
\end{equation}

The source spectra of particles accelerated by \SNRs thus follow a power law 
with spectral index $\gamma=\log\left(\frac{1}{1-P_\mathrm{esc.}}\right)\,/\,
\log\left(1+\langle\xi\rangle\right)$. For type II acceleration, the numeric 
value of $\gamma$ is determined by the cloud composition and extent, and tends 
to be very soft ($\gamma\gg1$) \cite{davisModifiedFermiMechanism1956}. For type
I acceleration, the spectral index can be rewritten in terms of the Mach number
$M=\beta c/v_\mathrm{sound}^\mathrm{ISM}$ of the shock front
\cite{gaisserCosmicRaysParticle2016}.

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:snr-spectral-index}
	\gamma \approx 1 + \frac{4}{M^2}
\end{equation}

Assuming strong shocks ($M\gg1$), \cref{eq:snr-spectral-index} is well 
approximated by $\gamma\approx1.7$, which, after accounting for diffusion 
effects ($\gamma_\mathrm{Earth}\approx\gamma_\mathrm{Source}+1$), matches the 
energy spectrum observed on earth below the knee almost perfectly 
\cite{baloghPhysicsCollisionlessShocks2013}.

\subsubsection{Injection \& Escape}

In order for acceleration to occur the particle must enter the regime of moving
magnetic fields, and cross into the shock front. As laid out in 
\cite{baloghPhysicsCollisionlessShocks2013} this requires a kinetic energy that
surpasses the thermal motion of particles in the \ISM ahead of the shock. There
is thus some need for a mechanism that gives them enough energy to cross the
shock front and be injected into the accelerator.

While many injection models exist (e.g.\ Ref.\ 
\cite{galeevInjectionMechanismCosmic1989}), the precise mechanism remains
unclear, which is referred to as the injection problem 
\cite{baloghPhysicsCollisionlessShocks2013}. In any case, data shows that a 
reliable injection mechanism exists, and particles can and do cross the shock 
front to enter into the acceleration cycle. The maximum energy that a given \CR
can attain is not constant, because $\frac{\mathrm{d}\beta}{\mathrm{dt}}<0$ and
$\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{dt}}>0$ as the \SNR ages.

Inspecting \cref{eq:energy-per-crossing}, the energy of the \CR particle during
acceleration grows according to the period length of one crossing.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:energy-budget}
\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{dt}} = \frac{\langle\xi\rangle\cdot E}{T_\mathrm{Cycle}}.
\end{equation}

This duration consists of an inscattering phase (in the upstream region of the 
shock), and an outscattering phase (downstream, after the shock). In both 
cases, the particle propagates in a quasi-random manner based on the 
irregularities present in the \EM fields near the \SNR. Following 
\cite{lagageMaximumEnergyCosmic1983} this implies

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:snr-cycle}
T_\mathrm{Cycle} = \frac{4}{c^2} \left( \frac{D_\mathrm{upstream}}{\beta_\mathrm{upstream}} + \frac{D_\mathrm{downstream}}{\beta_\mathrm{downstream}} \right).
\end{equation}

During the free expansion phase of the \SNR the flow velocities remain nearly 
constant. Thus, efficient acceleration is achieved for small diffusion 
coefficients $D_i$. A lower limit for $D_i$ is given by the Larmor radius 
$R_L$, as the particle cannot be deflected by magnetic fields through which 
it moves balistically \cite{lagageMaximumEnergyCosmic1983}. With 
$D_\mathrm{min} = \frac{R_L\,c}{3}$ and again assuming a strong shock 
($\beta_\mathrm{upstream} = 4\beta_\mathrm{downstream}$), integrating 
\cref{eq:energy-budget} over the duration of the free expansion phase leads to

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:snr-max-energy}
E_\mathrm{max} \leq \frac{3}{20} \int\limits_0^{T}\langle\xi\rangle q c^2 \left|\vec{B}\right| \beta_\mathrm{upstream}\, \mathrm{dE}.
\end{equation}

Interestingly, this leads to a maximum energy that only depends on the charge 
of the injected particle, not its initial energy. The higher total energy gain 
per cycle is offset by a longer cycle duration 
$T_\mathrm{Cycle} \propto E^{-1}$.

Inserting realistic parameters from observations, the maximum energy for \SNRs
according to \cref{eq:snr-max-energy} is \SI{1}{\peta\eV}, in line with 
predictions by the Hillas-criterion.

\subsection{Active Galactic Nuclei}
\label{ssec:active-galactic-nuclei}

Most (if not all) large galaxies host a \SMBH at their center
\cite{kormendyInwardBoundSearch1995, kormendyCoevolutionNotSupermassive2013}. 
Following the no-hair-theorem (Ref.\ \cite{carterAxisymmetricBlackHole1971}), 
these \SMBHs should, assuming a similar cosmogenic evolution, not differ 
strongly. This does not fit to observational evidence. A subsection of 
galaxies display a luminosity that far surpasses the median galactic luminosity
measured throughout the universe. Following the emission activity of the 
central region, the central black hole is called an \AGN.

The object responsible for this energy discharge must be the \SMBH, as the 
observed luminosity exceeds the Eddington limit, and cannot be explained by 
stellar processes alone \cite{rybickiRadiativeProcessesAstrophysics2024}. 
Instead, gravitational collapse is theorized to power the strong emission.
Particles scatter inelastically in the accretion disk surrounding the \SMBH.
The gravitational potential of one particle is passed onto a second particle, 
which escapes the central region at a higher energy. Such processes can be 
enhanced by the presence of strong magnetic fields with strengths up to 
\SI{1}{\mega\gauss} \cite{piotrovichDeterminationMagneticField2020}.
 
\subsubsection{Electromagnetic Centrifugal Acceleration}
\label{sssec:centrifugal-acceleration}

In particular, rotating magnetic field lines (caused by \SMBH rotating with an 
angular velocity $\Omega$) forc charged particles orbiting at a distance $r$ to
undergo centrifugal acceleration. This leads to a resonant interaction in which
rotational energy from the \SMBH is transferred to the particle like 

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:centrifugal-acceleration}
	E(r) = \frac{E_0}{1-\left(\frac{\Omega r}{c}\right)^2},
\end{equation}

as calculated in \cite{riegerParticleAccelerationRotating1999}. Clearly, 
$\lim\limits_{\Omega r\to c} E(r) = \infty$, implying that the 
outspiraling must be stopped at some point. In fact, gravitational friction
above and below the accretion disk produces a strong photon packground in which
accelerating particles lose energy via \ICS. This limits the maximum attainable
energy of (direct) centrigfugal acceleration in \AGNs 
to $\mathcal{O}(\unit{\exa\eV})$ 
\cite{osmanovEfficiencyParticleAcceleration2007}.

\subsubsection{Langmuir Damping \& Wave Collapse}

The plasma surrounding the \AGN can function as a catalyst for an immense 
acceleration of cosmic rays \cite{osmanovExtremelyEfficientZevatron2014}. 
Expanding from \cref{eq:centrifugal-acceleration}, it is evident that electrons
and ions undergo acceleration at different rates. This results in large-scale 
charge separation within the plasma, and the formation of Langmuir waves. 

Initially, the plasma waves have a large wavelength, and, because 
$v_\mathrm{Phase} \propto \omega/\lambda$, a very high phase velocity 
$v_\mathrm{Phase}>c$. Since the charge carriers cannot match this phase 
velocity, Landau damping (Ref.\ \cite{landau61VibrationsElectronic1965}) is 
effectively prevented. The process continues and rotational energy of the
magnetic field is transferred into plasma wave energy, the amplitude of the
Langmuir waves grows.

This is only stopped once the amplitude is big enough that wave-driven pressure
differences cause the collapse of low-pressure cavities. The plasma wavelength 
rapidly decreases because of this cave-in and the phase velocity becomes 
sub-luminal. Suddenly, an overlap between particle speeds and the plasma phase 
velocity enables Landau damping, and wave energy is passed onto the charge 
carriers. For protons in particular the energy after collapse becomes

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:langmuir-acceleration}
	E \approx 6.4\times10^{112}\,\, \frac{f^3}{\gamma_2^5} \,\,
	\sqrt{\left(
		\frac{M_\odot}{M_\mathrm{AGN}}
		\frac{L_\mathrm{AGN}}{\unit{erg\per\second}} 
	      \right)^5}\,\mathrm{eV}.
\end{equation}

In \cref{eq:langmuir-acceleration} $f=\delta n/n_0$ denotes the magnitude of 
irregularities $\delta n$ deviating from the nominal plasma density $n_0$. 
$\gamma_2$ is the Lorentz factor of the ion plasma stream, $M_\mathrm{AGN}$ and
$L_\mathrm{AGN}$ are the mass and luminosity of the \AGN respectively. 
Observations of the plasma conditions in \AGNs place a realistic upper limit
of this acceleration mechanism at 
$\mathcal{O}(\unit{Z\eV} = \SI{1e21}{\eV})$.

\subsection{Indirect Production}
\label{ssec:indirect-production}

High-energy photons and neutrinos \CRs are observed in nature 
\cite{lhaasocollaborationUltrahighenergyRayBubble2024,
aielloObservationUltrahighenergyCosmic2025}, but their existence cannot be 
explained using the acceleration mechanisms disussed previously. Being neutral 
particles, photons and neutrinos do not couple to the electromagnetic fields 
that charged \CRs rely on to gain immense energies.

A straight-forward solution to this problem is given via indirect production of
neutral \CRs. This refers to the various interaction channels by which a 
high-energy charged \CR can transfer (part of) its' energy to a secondary, 
uncharged \CR.

\subsubsection{Synchrotron Emission}

Whenever a charged particle changes its' trajectory, the surrounding 
electromagnetic fields change as well. A distant observer measures a 
retarded potential stemming from a particle moving at $\vec{v_1}$ initially, 
and $\vec{v_2}$ finally. The change in the electromagnetic fields is perceived 
as photons whose wavelength and intensity scale with the magnitude of the 
acceleration. This phenomenon is called Bremsstrahlung.

\CR electrons, because of their low mass, are especially prone to acceleration 
via \EM fields, both near the source, and during propagation via the \GMF. Near
the source in particular, the charged particles often move relativistically in 
a circular pattern (c.f.\ \cref{sssec:centrifugal-acceleration}). Under such
circumstances the (by the electron) isotropically emitted Bremsstrahlung is 
boosted into the direction of the electron motion. The brilliance and
thus intensity of the light beam increases with the Lorentz factor of the 
electron \cite{rybickiRadiativeProcessesAstrophysics2024}. This is called 
synchrotron radiation, and is an important production mechanism for photons 
with energies up to few \unit{\kilo\eV} \cite{gaisserCosmicRaysParticle2016} 
around e.g.\ pulsars, or accreting black holes.

\subsubsection{Deep inelastic scattering}
\label{sssec:deep-inelastic-scattering}

Deep inelastic scattering as studied at the \LHC also occurs readily in nature,
with \CRs interacting with the \ISM. The invariant mass of the colliding system
can exceed that of the \LHC ($\SI{14}{\tera\eV}$) by many orders of magnitude. 
In the simplest case a high-energy proton interacts with ambient (stationary) 
matter. The interaction channels with the highest branching ratio are those
that produce light pions.

\section{Cosmic Ray Propagation}
\label{sec:cr-propagation}

The propagation of cosmic rays refers to their galactic and extragalactic 
transport from a source region to Earth. This cosmic play occurs over 
timescales measuring hundreds of thousands of years. The stage through which 
the \CR actors propagate is set up by various magnetic fields, as presented in 
\cref{ssec:cosmic-magnetic-fields}. Different effects that can occur during the
transport are highlighted in \cref{ssec:galactic-transport}.

\subsection{Cosmic Magnetic Fields}
\label{ssec:cosmic-magnetic-fields}

\halflift

\subsubsection{Coherent Galactic Component}

The milky way, like many spiral galaxies \cite{beckMagneticFieldsSpiral2016}, 
supports an orderly magnetic field that permeates the entire galaxy. This 
coherent component of the \GMF is thought to originate from primordial seed 
fields that are amplified by processes such as small scale dynamos 
\cite{kimInterstellarTurbulenceDriving2006}. The galactic evolution then 
follows these seed fields. Newly formed structures tend to align with the field
lines, and a self-reinforcing process kicks off that leads to large-scale \EM 
fields with strength of several \unit{\upmu\gauss} 
\cite{wangMagnetohydrodynamicSimulationsDisk2009, 
kotarbaMagneticFieldStructure2009, gresselHybridDynamoModel2013}.
\cref{fig:coherent-magnetic-field} shows the magnetic flux density of the 
coherent galactic magnetic field.

\subsubsection{Turbulent Galactic Component}

The turbulent component of the \GMF is the difference between the ordered 
component discussed above and the total \GMF. Its' coherence length measures 
typically $L_\mathrm{C} \leq \SI{5}{\parsec}$ 
\cite{giacintiConstraintsPropertiesTurbulent2019}, and can be separated into 
many individual Weissian domains that are oriented in a quasi-random manner.
With field strenghts up to \SI{6}{\upmu\gauss} 
\cite{hanSpatialEnergySpectrum2004} the turbulent \GMF represents a 
considerable fraction of the total galactic magnetic field, stronger than the 
coherent component in many regions. The chaotic orientation complicates \CR 
propagation to a (transient) random walk in three dimensions. 
\cref{fig:flux-demagnification} shows the flux demagnification of \CR arrival 
directions due to the \GMF and its' turbulent component in particular.

\subsubsection{Extragalactic Component}

The \EGMF influences the transport of \CRs between galaxies. Given the 
relatively weak (estimated) strength of the \EGMF (ranging from 
$\sim\SI{1}{\nano\gauss}$ \cite{brayUpperLimitStrength2018} to 
$\sim\SI{1}{\upmu\gauss}$ \cite{valleeDetectingLargestMagnets1990}), as well as
the relatively high rigidity of extragalactic \CRs (Ref.\ \cref{sssec:energy}),
it is usually assumed that \CRs travel balistically in-between galaxies;
Interactions with the \EGMF are neglected. 

This has implications for the identification and search of extragalactic 
sources of \UHECRs. A well known example of this is Amaterasu
\cite{telescopearraycollaborationExtremelyEnergeticCosmic2023}, an \EAS with an
associated reconstructed primary energy of $E\approx\SI{240}{\exa\eV}$. 
Neglecting the \EGMF, conventional backtracking algorithms place the most 
probable origin of the primary particle in the local void 
\cite{ungerWhereDidAmaterasu2024}, a region sparse in \UHECR sources 
\cite{tullyNearbyGalaxiesCatalog1988}, and require transient production 
mechanisms which are not yet thoroughly understood at the highest energies 
\cite{farrarBinaryNeutronStar2025}. Meanwhile, assuming a non-zero \EGMF,
\cite{bourricheLocalVoidComprehensive2024}\todo{change to updated version} constrains the source location to 
a region with several candidates that can produce particles at the required
energies. The question whether the \EGMF plays a considerable role in \CR 
propagation is unanswered, and requires a better understanding of their 
dynamics.

\subsection{Galactic Transport}
\label{ssec:galactic-transport}

The transport of \CRs in the milky way is goverened by many different 
processes. In general, these processes are of stochastic nature. The 
examination of the trajectory of a single \CR thus only gives an incomplete 
picture of the many phenomena that can occur during transport. It is more 
instructive to consider a population of \CRs (of type $i$) and study how the 
entire population evolves in space and time. Without loss of generality, it is 
evident that the (diffusive) magnetic transport along the turbulent component 
of the \GMF conserves the number of particles $\mathcal{N}_i$,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:particle-conservation}
\frac{\partial\mathcal{N}_i}{\partial t} - \nabla \cdot \bigg[\,\widetilde{D}_i(E,\vec{r}, t)\,\nabla\mathcal{N}_i\,\bigg] = 0.
\end{equation}

$\widetilde{D}_i(E,\vec{r}, t)$ in \cref{eq:particle-conservation} gives the 
directionally depependent diffusion coefficients at point $\vec{r}$ and time 
$t$ for particles of type $i$. Because the driving force powering the diffusion
is the Lorentz force, $\widetilde{D}$ is rigidity- and thus energy-dependent, 
with higher energy \CRs diffusing quicker than lower energy \CRs.

The diffusion only models the adiabatic transport of preexisting cosmic rays.
Their creation can be described by adding a source term $Q_i(E, \vec{r}, t)$. 
For example, the prompt emission of \UHE photons during a \GRB can be encoded 
as

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:grb-source-term}
Q_\gamma(E, \vec{r}, t) = N_\gamma(E) \, \delta(\vec{r}-\vec{r}_\mathrm{GRB}) \, \delta(t-t_\mathrm{GRB}).
\end{equation}

During transport of particles of type $i$ the production of secondary \CRs of 
type $j\neq i$ can occur (Ref.\ \cref{ssec:indirect-production}), the 
production rate is tied to the scattering cross section $\sigma_{ij}$ 
describing the interaction $p_\mathrm{ISM} + \CR_i \rightarrow X + \CR_j$
\footnote{For weakly unstable primaries decay can occur in vacuum as well.}, 
the initial \CR is destroyed in the process and $\mathcal{N}_i$ decreases as a 
consequence.

This also happens in reverse, and \CRs of type $i$ are produced in secondary 
interactions. In equilibrium, the population size during transport remains 
constant, implying that globally

\begin{align}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{N}_i}{\partial t} &= \int Q_i \, \mathrm{d}\vec{r} \nonumber \\
					    &\qquad + \rho_\mathrm{ISM} \, \sum\limits_{j\neq i} \Bigg(\underbrace{\int \frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{ji}(E, E')}{\mathrm{d}E} |\vec{v}_j| \, \mathcal{N}_j \, \mathrm{d}E' \vphantom{\Bigg[}}_\mathrm{Secondary\,production} - \underbrace{\sigma_{ij}(E) \, |\vec{v}_i|\, \mathcal{N}_i \vphantom{\Bigg[}}_\mathrm{Primary\,destruction} \Bigg) \nonumber \\
					    &\qquad + \sum\limits_{j\neq i} \Bigg(\underbrace{\lambda_{ji}\,\mathcal{N}_j - \lambda_{ij}\,\mathcal{N}_i \vphantom{\Bigg[}}_\mathrm{Weak\,decay}\Bigg) \nonumber \\
					    &= 0. \label{eq:secondary-equilibrium}
\end{align}

Finally, \CRs can thermalize during transport via e.g.\ inelastic scattering
processes. An example of this is the interactions of \UHECRs with the \CMB 
covered in \cref{sssec:gzk-cutoff}. The energy loss models
$\frac{\partial k_i}{\partial E} $ must be tailored for each \CR type 
individually, as different primaries can posses different interaction channels.

Combining \cref{eq:particle-conservation} through 
(\ref{eq:secondary-equilibrium}), one arrives at a system of parabolic partial 
differential equations that are in practice solved numerically by tools such as 
\texttt{CRPropa} \cite{batistaCRPropa32Advanced2022} or \texttt{GALPROP}
\cite{strongGALPROPCosmicRayPropagation2009}. \cref{fig:galactic-propagation} 
shows the calculated trajectory of particles of different rigidities in the 
milky way, as well as their perceived origin.

\subsection{(Nested) Leaky Box Model}
\label{ssec:leaky-box-model}

One can in principle solve the equation of state analytically for extremely
simplified boundary conditions. In an illustrative example we neglect all 
secondary interactions of \CRs during transport.\footnote{Meaning spallation, 
radioactive decay, and energy losses.} Due to the oblate shape of the milky way
it is sufficient to reduce calculations to the 1D case along the $z$-axis. The 
parameters of interest are \cite{skillingDiffusionCosmicRays1970}

\begin{equation}
	Q(z, t) = \mathcal{N}_0 \, \delta(z)\delta(t), \qquad D_z = \frac{1}{3} \,\frac{\gamma|\vec{B}|L_{\vec{B}}}{\sqrt{\mu_0\rho_\mathrm{ISM}}},
\end{equation}

and a particular solution to this initial value problem is given by

\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{N}(z, t) = \frac{\mathcal{N}_0}{\sqrt{4\pi\,D_z t}} \, e^{-z^2/(2D_z t)}.
\end{equation}

The fraction of particles escaping the galaxy after time $t$ can be found by
integrating the \CR population along the height of the milky way. From this,
the expected time to escape for an individual \CR is calculated as

\begin{equation}
	\langle\frac{1}{\tau_\mathrm{esc.}}\rangle = \int t \, r_\mathrm{esc}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t = \int t \, \left( 1 - \int\limits_{-H}^{H} \frac{\mathcal{N}(z, t)}{\mathcal{N}_0}\,\mathrm{d}z \right) \, \mathrm{d}t
\end{equation}

The full set of partial differential equations can be drastically simplified 
with a related Ansatz. The diffusion term, secondary production and destruction
are modeled stochastically via mean free path lengths and average lifetimes. 
The equation of state in equilibrium becomes

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:leaky-box}
\frac{\mathcal{N}_i}{\tau_\mathrm{esc.}} = Q_i - \left( \frac{|\vec{v}_i|\,\rho_\mathrm{ISM}}{\lambda_i} + \frac{1}{\gamma_i\tau_i}\right)\,\mathcal{N}_i + \sum\limits_{j\neq i} \left( \frac{|\vec{v}_j|\rho_\mathrm{ISM}}{\lambda_{ji}}  + \frac{1}{\gamma_j\tau_{ji}}\right)\,\mathcal{N}_j.
\end{equation}

This is called the leaky box model, and serves as a reasonable approximation 
for a steady-state solution of \CR transport. \cref{fig:escape-comparison} 
shows the comparison between the mean escape time in the leaky box model and 
the simulated escape time using numerical \CR propagation algorithms.

\section{Extensive Air Showers}
\label{sec:extensive-air-showers}

The creation and transport of \CRs has been extensively discussed in
\cref{sec:cr-accelerators} and \cref{sec:cr-propagation} respectively. In this
section the final stage of the life of a cosmic ray in the upper atmosphere of 
the Earth is summarized. For this purpose, \cref{ssec:cascade-model} details
the formation and evolution of \EASs. The possible detection channels through
which \EASs can be observed are presented in \cref{ssec:particle-signature} and
onwards.

\subsection{Cascade Physics}
\label{ssec:cascade-model}

Whereas \CRs can propagate almost unhindered in space, crossing a column 
density of only $\lesssim\SI{8}{\gram\per\centi\meter\squared}$ on average
before escaping \cite{gaisserCosmicRaysParticle2016}, this quickly changes upon
entering the atmosphere of Earth. The mean free path of the \CR decreases 
drastically due to the high(er) density surrounding, and a deeply inelastic 
interaction takes place in which the primary particle is destroyed. The \UHE 
secondary particles, carrying the momentum of the impinging \CR, suffer a 
similar fate and also interact non-elastically with surrounding air molecules. 
A particle cascade kicks off that is only stopped once the primary energy is 
distributed to so many particles that they can become thermalized by secondary
interactions.

\subsubsection{Heitler-Matthews-Model}
\label{sssec:heitler-matthews-model}

A simple but effective model to describe the avalanche is given by
\cite{matthewsHeitlerModelExtensive2005}. The evolution of the \EAS can thus be
separated into discrete steps. In the first step, the initial particle produces
$N_\pi$ pions, with the primary energy $E_0$ being shared roughly equally among
them. Since the branching ratios for neutral and charged pions are roughly 
equal, this amounts to $\frac{2N_\pi}{3}$ charged $\pi^\pm$ and 
$\frac{N_\pi}{3}$ uncharged $\pi^0$. 

The lifetime of the uncharged pions ($\mathcal{O}(\unit{\atto\second})$ 
\cite{nakamuraParticlePhysicsBooklet2010}) is much shorter than that of the 
charged pions ($\mathcal{O}(\unit{\nano\second})$ 
\cite{nakamuraParticlePhysicsBooklet2010}). In the context of the 
Heitler-Matthews model they decay instantly into two equi-energetic photons.

Moving on, it is sensible to separate the \EAS into a hadronic and an \EM
component. The charged pions, after traversing an atmospheric depth equal to 
one nuclear interaction length $\lambda_\mathrm{int}.$ produce another $N_\pi$
pions each, where the uncharged portion immediately decays and the charged 
portion fuels the hadronic cascade.

The photons from the $\pi^0$ decays are responsible for driving the \EM 
component of the shower. After traversing close to one radiation length 
$\frac{7}{9} X_0$ ($\neq \lambda_\mathrm{int.}$) the high-energy photons 
undergo pair production, resulting in an $e^+ e^-$ pair, which, after 
traversing another $X_0$ produce a photon each via bremsstrahlung. Throughout 
all these processes it is assumed that the energy of the parent particle is 
equally distributed to the child particles. The schematic evolution is 
visualized in \cref{fig:heitler-matthews-cascade}.

The number of particles in the cascade grows exponentially. After only five 
hadronic interaction lengths ($\approx20\,X_0$) the number of particles
in the shower is $\mathcal{O}(10^6)$. Energy conservation requires that the 
energy per particle drops consequently. Eventually, the one-particle-energy 
drops below a critical energy ($E^\mathrm{crit.}_\mathrm{EM}$ for $e^\pm$, 
and $\upgamma$; $E^\mathrm{crit.}_\mathrm{H}$ for hadronic particles), where 
ionization processes become the dominant interaction channel. The cascade 
ceases multiplying, and the hadronic component decays to relativistic muons.
The \EM component is absorbed or thermalized. The shower evolution parameters 
modeling this are listed in \cref{tab:shower-parameters}.

\end{equation}

This relation is exact in the Heitler model, i.\ e.\ for electrically neutral
particles \cite{HeitlerQuantumTheory}. Although shower-to-shower fluctuations 
play a larger role for charged primaries, \cref{eq:shower-maximum} generalizes 
especially for hadronic primaries as well \cite{gaisserCosmicRaysParticle2016,
matthewsHeitlerModelExtensive2005}.

Moving past characteristics of the primary particle (energy, inclination, and 
type), one parameter describing the developing cascade well is the shower age 
\cite{rossiCosmicRayTheory1941}, 

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:shower-age}
\bar{s}(X, \Xmax) = \frac{3X}{X + 2\Xmax}\ .
\end{equation}

Like the $x$-axis in \cref{fig:heitler-matthews-cascade}, $\bar{s}$ 
monotonically increases with the distance from the first interaction point.
Using this analogy, it is easy to see that energy distribution of particles is
almost entirely characterized by $\bar{s}$.\footnote{Dependencies on $E_0$ are
absorbed by defining the shower age in terms of $\Xmax$.} Similar connections 
are found for the angular and lateral distribution of particles in the \EAS
\cite{rossiCosmicRayTheory1941}, with both functions tending to broaden as the 
shower age increases.

What sets individual showers apart are, following this shower universality 
theory, tiny deviations that get amplified during the buildup of the 
relativistic avalanche. The exact kinematic variables of few high-energy 
particles (which may not follow the mean kinematic variables in the shower 
bulk) at an early stage are passed onto the shower bulk at an older shower age.


\subsubsection{Hadronic Interaction Models}
\label{sssec:hadronic-interaction-models}

A feeling for the size of shower-to-shower fluctuations can be gathered from 
bootstrapping simulations of the same \CR primary particle interacting with the
atmosphere. Multiple codes and frameworks exist that aim to balance the 
physical accuracy of the reenactment versus the required computational power. A
(non-exhaustive) list of different models is given here in no particular order:

\begin{itemize}
\vspace{0.1cm}
\item \texttt{QGSJET-II} \cite{ostapchenkoMonteCarloTreatment2011} relies on 
extrapolating predictions of \QFTs to the highest energies for phenomena such 
as screening and multiple scattering. The models theoretical approach also has 
drawbacks. The complex calculations result in a time intensive simulation 
process. Moreover, the extrapolation of theoretical predictions is not perfect,
\texttt{QGSJET-II} predicts a lower number of muons than what is observed in 
air shower experiments.

\vspace{0.1cm}
\item \texttt{EPOS LHC} \cite{pierogEPOSLHCTest2015} (and the revised 
\texttt{EPOS LHC R} \cite{pierogEPOSLHCRGlobalApproach2025}) aims to accurately
model the central and forward particle production, that, while still 
underpredicting data by $\sim\SI{30}{\percent}$, result in more realistic muon 
yields. The model is slightly more performant than \texttt{QGSJET-II.04}

\vspace{0.1cm}
\item \texttt{Sibyll} \cite{riehnHadronicInteractionModel2017} is an efficient
\MC based simulation framework designed with tuneability in mind. The models
versatility makes it extremely useful in studies of the muonic component of
\EASs.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Hadronic particles}

Hadronic primaries refer to \CR particles bound by the (residual) strong force.
The lightest hadronic primaries observed in nature are thus hydrogen ions 
(i.e.\ protons) with an atomic number $Z=1$ for which the developing cascade 
has been described in \cref{ssec:cascade-model}. Showers initiated by heavier 
elements behave differently on average compared to the 1-nucleon model. 

The average binding energy of nucleons is $\mathcal{O}(\unit{\mega\eV})$, and 
much lower than the interaction energies. As such, the \CR with a mass number
$A$ can be treated as $A$ \CRs, each with an energy $E_0/A$. The resulting \EAS
is a superposition of all these 1-nucleon showers. From 
\cref{eq:shower-maximum} it follows immediately that

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:superposition-model}
\Xmax \propto \ln E_0/A.
\end{equation}

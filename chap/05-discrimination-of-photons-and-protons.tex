\section{Monte Carlo Datasets}

Confirmed photon \CRs, detected e.g.~by the \LHAASO have reconstructed primary 
energies of $<\SI{10}{\peta\eV}$ 
\cite{lhaasocollaborationUltrahighenergyRayBubble2024}, less than 
\SI{10}{\percent} of the lower bound on the energy considered in this work. 
No photon candidates have ever been measured in the search space studied here.

With this lack of experimental evidence we must base our analysis on 
simulations of \EASs. The following sections detail how these simulations are 
performed, and present the datasets of simulated air showers used throughout 
\cref{chap:chapter-4}.

The code used in generating the simulation datasets are available at 
Ref.~\cite{AugerobservatorySandBoxesPaulfilip2026}.

\subsection{Air Shower Simulation}

\texttt{CORSIKA v7.8010} \cite{engelNextGenerationCORSIKA2018} is used to 
simulate air showers for both photon and proton primaries. In either case, 
primary particles with a true energy $E_\mathrm{MC}$ are injected with a 
heading $(\phi_\mathrm{MC},\,\theta_\mathrm{MC})$ into the upper atmosphere,
and the resulting particle cascade is simulated using \texttt{EPOS\_LHC} 
(Ref.~\cref{sssec:hadronic-interaction-models}, 
\cite{pierogEPOSLHCTest2015}), with \texttt{FLUKA  2011.2c} 
\cite{theflukacollaborationFLUKACodeOverview2024} handling particle 
interactions below a center-of-mass energy of \SI{80}{\giga\eV}.

\subsubsection{Particle Distribution}

Particles are injected at an atmospheric depth of 
\SI{0}{\gram\per\centi\meter\squared}, with the parametrized air density 
profile emulating typically measured atmospheric conditions in Malargüe in 
September, as described in further detail in 
Ref.~\cite{abreuDescriptionAtmosphericConditions2012}. 

The heading of the primary particle is randomized with the distribution of 
$(\phi_\mathrm{MC},\,\theta_\mathrm{MC})$ mimicking a uniform flux over 
the sky as observed by a flat detector. Specifically,
$\phi_\mathrm{MC}\sim\mathcal{U}(\SI{-180}{\degree},\,\SI{180}{\degree})$,
and $\sin(2\,\theta_\mathrm{MC})\sim\mathcal{U}(0,\,0.766)$. The upper 
bound on the latter parameter corresponds to a maximum simulated zenith 
angle of $\SI{65}{\degree}$. The distribution of simulated primary zenith 
angles is shown in \cref{fig:zenith-mc-distribution}.

The primary energy of injected particles is 
$\log_{10}\left(E_\mathrm{MC}\,/\,\mathrm{eV}\right)\in[16.5, 19.0]$, 
covering a broad range from full inefficiency,\footnote{Meaning no \EAS 
below this energy is ever observed in the \SD-750.} to an upper energy
where the size of the array becomes a bottleneck in detecting the faint
\CR fluxes. For an individual \texttt{CORSIKA} run the energy range of the 
particle is fixed to one tenth of a decade in logarithmic energy. Within 
this bin, the energy is randomized such that 
$E^\mathrm{bin}_\mathrm{MC}\sim E$, meaning that higher values are picked 
more likely than lower values. In order to obtain sufficient sample sizes 
across all energy bins lower energy showers are simulated more frequently 
than higher energy showers. Both considerations influence the distribution 
of simulated primary energies, as shown in 
\cref{fig:energy-mc-distribution}.

In order to be computationally efficient the simulation framework employs 
\emph{thinning} \cite{saffiAnalysisSimulationReconstruction2020}, which 
assigns weights to individual particles in the shower cascade. A particle 
with weight $W > 1$ consequently approximates $W$ copies of itself, 
reducing the number of actually simulated particles, and cutting down on 
processing time. The threshold for the thinning algorithm to group 
particles together is energy dependent. This threshold is set dynamically 
to an optimal value of $10^{-6}\,E_\mathrm{MC}$ 
\cite{billoirSamplingProcedureRegenerate2008}.

\subsubsection{Detector Signal}

The simulation of the shower cascade halts at an atmospheric depth 
corresponding to an altitude of $\SI{1400}{\meter}\,\mathrm{a.s.l.}$, 
the height of the \PAOs detectors. At this point, the properties of the 
remaining particles in the simulated shower front are handed to an
\Offline simulation that generates (\SD) detector signals from the 
particle distribution on ground.

First, the point of intersection between the shower axis (fixed by 
$\phi_\mathrm{MC},\,\theta_\mathrm{MC}$) and the detector plane is
determined. We pick a random point in a unit rectangle with a random 
\SD-750 station at its' center to mirror the expected 
uniform\footnote{Non-uniformities are caused by detector 
inefficiencies, see e.g.~Ref.~\cref{TODO}} shower core distribution on
the surface of the Earth. Once this point is picked, the entire geometry 
of shower + detector is fixed. Particles (with weight $W$) that fall within
a predefined volume around an \SD-station are \emph{dethinned} 
\cite{saffiAnalysisSimulationReconstruction2020}, and particle trajectories
through the \WCD, \SSD, and \UMD, as well as resulting material 
interactions are simulated using \texttt{Geant4} 
\cite{allisonRecentDevelopmentsGeant42016, 
allisonGeant4DevelopmentsApplications2006, 
agostinelliGeant4aSimulationToolkit2003}. The trajectory of light created 
in the scintillating material of the \SSD and \UMD, as well as in the 
Cherenkov cone of relativistic muons in the \WCD is propagated to the 
different sensor components, eventually resulting a simulated detector 
response to the virtual particle flux. On top of this, electronic noise 
induced by the \DAQ readout chain, as well as low-energy atmospheric 
background muons are injected into the detector simulation to increase 
the degree of realism of the calculations.

\subsection{Event Reconstruction}

The simulated detector response from the \WCD, \SSD, and \UMD is 
reconstructed according to the standard reconstruction procedure defined
in \cref{ssec:sd-reconstruction} and \cref{ssec:umd-reconstruction}.

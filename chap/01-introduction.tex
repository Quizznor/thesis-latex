\label{chap:introduction}

\setlength{\parindent}{20pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{2pt}

Roughly ten years ago, I, the author of this thesis, had just started my 
undergraduate studies of physics at the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology. I was
trying to keep up with all the different lectures I attended. Sometimes the work
load would be overwhelming. Then, I would go for a walk at night. I would find a
bench to sit down on, and look up into the night sky to calm my mind.

This all seems impossibly far back to me now. I invite you to think back to what
your life was like ten years ago. So many things have changed. Some have changed
for the better, and some have changed for the worse. In a less personal sense, 
the world has shifted drastically in a relatively short time. Moving past the 
ten year mark, the story of humanities technological advance is nothing short 
of awe-inspiring. A person born in the late 19th century grew up before the 
invention of airplanes. Some 60 years later, the first human set foot on the 
moon. 60 years later still, the processing capabilities in a commercial 
smartphone far outperform those of the Apollo 11 navigation computers.

What would Magellan or Columbus say to GPS sattelites? Would the wanderings of 
Odysseus have been a similar epos had he had access to a sextant? Our 
ever-evolving culture and knowledge of the world makes it impossible to answer 
this today. Still, despite all the contrast throughout time and space, there 
exists one thing that unites you and me with Magellan, with Aristotle, with 
everyone.

Since the beginning of documented history we have looked to the stars. Whether
it be for spiritual guidance or earthly navigation, the twinkling image of 
faraway worlds has inspired a sense of wonder in us. We are curious about the 
cosmos, and want to know how things function. This curiosity makes us human, and
is the driving force behind our technological prowess.

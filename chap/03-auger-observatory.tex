\chapter{The Pierre Auger Observatory}
\label{chap:pierre-auger-observatory}

The \PAO is the largest scientific experiment in the world, spanning an area of
roughly \SI{3000}{\kilo\meter\squared}. It consists of an array of 1660 \WCDs, 
which form the \SD, and 27 fluorescence telescopes, spread over five locations,
that comprise the \FD. All data from \SD stations, \FD telescopes as well as 
numerous atmospheric monitoring sites converge at a single location, the \CDAS
in the nearby city of Malargüe in Argentina. 

The observatory is designed to indirectly detect \CRs of incredibly high 
energies, inferring their existence from the observation of large particle 
cascades in the atmosphere. The simultaneous operation of the \FD and the \SD 
offers a unique possibility to observe \UHECRs at the tail-end of the \CR 
energy spectrum, and makes the \PAO world-leading in the area of \UHECR 
research.

This chapter introduces the observatory and the collaboration operating it. A 
summary of the project history and science case is given in 
\cref{sec:project-history-and-future}. \cref{sec:fd}, and \cref{sec:sd} detail 
the hardware components of the \FD and the \SD respectively. Finally, 
the analysis chain from \EAS detection to the estimation of physics parameters 
during the event reconstruction is examined in \cref{sec:event-reconstruction}.

\section{Project History and Future}
\label{sec:project-history-and-future}

The project that is today known as the \acl{PAO} looks back on a history which 
spans more than three decades in total. First plans for a \CR detector of
immense size were devised by Nobel laureate James Cronin 
\cite{nobelprizeoutreach2025NobelPrizePhysics} and Faraday medalist Alan Watson
\cite{FellowWinsIoP2011} at the 22nd \ICRC held in August of 1991 in Dublin,
Ireland \cite{watsonDevelopmentPierreAuger}.

The two colleagues realized that the flux of \UHECRs is very low, averaging
only about one particle per square kilometer and year for energies above 
\SI{10}{\exa\eV} \cite{fenuCosmicRayEnergy2023}), and that one consequently 
needs to observe a large area over a long timespan in order to collect enough 
data for a statistically relevant analysis of \UHECR dynamics. Cronin 
especially was interested in verifying the possible verification of an 
astrophysical source of high-energy $\gamma$-rays 
\cite{samorskiDetection2101983}. Moreover, the mere existence of some \UHECRs 
was challenging astrophysical models 
\cite{birdDetectionCosmicRay1995, naganoAstrophysicalAspectsMost1991, 
hayashidaObservationVeryEnergetic1994, lawrenceCosmicRayEnergy1991}, and 
urgently called for further investigation. 

Naturally, probing the presence and possible sources of \CRs of such extreme 
energies became a priority for the envisioned experiment. Cronin and Watson 
gathered support for their ideas and, in 1995, founded a collaboration of 140 
scientists from 17 participating countries. A white paper was published that 
outlined the design and capabilities of what would become the \acl{PAO} 
\cite{thepierreaugercollaborationPierreAugerObservatory1997}. 

The detector configuration presented in
\cite{thepierreaugercollaborationPierreAugerObservatory1997} varies from the 
design in use today. Most importantly, what was originally envisioned to 
be a detector with an active area of \SI{6000}{\km\squared} spread over two 
locations (Auger North and Auger South) had to be amended and only one site 
with and area of \SI{3000}{\km\squared}, located near Malargüe in the 
Argentinian Pampa Amarilla in the Mendoza province came to be. 

There, the construction of an Engineering Array, a full scale prototype of the 
first \SD and \FD detectors was completed in 2001. More hardware was added 
after first successful measurements, making the still incomplete detector the 
largest observatory for \CRs by the end of 2003. Interested researchers kept 
joining the ambitious project during the construction phase, boosting the 
member count of the Pierre Auger collaboration (as of January 2026) to 359 
co-authors in 19 countries.

The deployment of detectors finished by late 2008, and the observatory was 
officially inaugurated on the 14th of November in the same year 
\cite{InaugurationPierreAuger}, coinciding in time with the founding date of
its host city, with which the Pierre Auger collaboration has maintained a very 
close relationship over the years \cite{JamesCroninSchool, 
MalargueObservatorioPierre, NicolasLealMalarguino2025, 
allekotteMalargueStreets26}.

\subsection{Goals and Achievements}
\label{ssec:science-goals-and-achievements}

The \PAO has been operating almost uninterrupted ever since, collecting data 
that correspond to a total exposure well surpassing 
\SI{100000}{\km\squared\sr\year} \cite{aabPierreAugerObservatory2020}. Using
the collected events, the Pierre Auger Collaboration has delivered key insights
that advance our understanding of astroparticle and high-energy physics. Most 
notably, the \PAO confirmed a strong suppression of the \CR-flux for particles 
above $\SI{4e19}{\eV}$ \cite{yamamotoUHECRSpectrumMeasured2007}. Studies of the
event arrival directions hint to anisotropies and the extracalactic origin of 
\UHECRs \cite{thepierreaugercollaborationObservationLargescaleAnisotropy2017}.
Upper limits have been set on the \UHE photon and neutrino flux, excluding some
astrophysical models \cite{collaborationPierreAugerObservatory2011, 
abreuSearchUltrahighEnergy2011}. Last but not least, regular releases of 
datasets to the public support the work of independently working 
astrophysicists around the globe \cite{zavrtanikPierreAugerObservatory2025}.

\subsection{AugerPrime and Outlook}
\label{ssec:augerprime-and-outlook}

Many questions have been answered thanks to the data collected by the \PAO. 
Naturally, new insights sprout new questions. The existence of \UHECRs up to
the \GZK-cutoff has been established unambigously. Now, their nature must be 
studied more deeply. Of particular interest is the reconstruction of the mass
of \UHECRs, as this influences their (reconstructed 
\cite{yushkovMassCompositionCosmic2021}) energy, and evolution from the source
to the earth (Ref.\ \cref{sec:cr-propagation}, 
\cite{strongCosmicRayPropagationInteractions2007, 
flaggsStudyingMassSensitivity2024}). Extended information on the mass 
composition of \CRs will significantly advance the understanding of high-energy
physics in particular, and the cosmos in general.

The \PAO detectors have undergone a major upgrade in the past decade in order 
to increase the observatories sensitivity to the primary particle mass. This 
overhaul, called \textit{AugerPrime}, equips the \SD stations with several 
additional detection channels. The \DAQ chain was improved and new readout 
electronics have been developed to increase the fidelity and quality of 
collected data. The \FD duty cycle was improved, and new methods for data 
analysis are being tested.

As of the completion of this thesis, the AugerPrime hardware and software 
upgrade is completed and the current state of the \PAO is summarized on the 
following pages. An in-depth report can be found in
\cite{collaborationPierreAugerObservatory2011}. Overall, the observatory 
delivers data with lower systematics post-upgrade. Moreover, additional 
information from new detectors assists the interpretation of observed 
phenomena. 

The prospects of this detector upgrade have astroparticle physicists excited. 
An independent review in 2023 resulted in the strong support in the scientific 
community to extend the observatories funding until beyond the year 2030 
\cite{castellinaOutcomeFinanceBoard2023}. The Phase-II of \DAQ (as opposed to 
Phase-I pre- and during AugerPrime) will be the basis for many scientific 
contributions that attempt to shed light into the physics of \UHECRs, with this
work being just one example.

\section{The Fluorescence Detector}
\label{sec:fd}

The \acf{FD} of the \PAO is a set of 27 reflector telescopes tuned to detect 
faint sources of \UV light. More specifically, the aim of the \FD is to observe
the \UV-emission of \EAS (Ref.\ \cref{sssec:fluorescence-light}). Since the 
solar and even lunar irradiance far outshine even the brightest \EASs, the \FD
can only operate during \UV-quiet peroids \cite{mathesCriteriaFDShift}. This 
drops the duty cycle to approximately 13\% 
\cite{abrahamFluorescenceDetectorPierre2010}. Despite this drastic limitation, 
the \FD is a crucial component for the analysis of \EASs, as it can directly 
observe the longitudinal profile of an air shower.

The telescopes are grouped at four \FD sites, each supporting a collection of 
six identical setups that offer a $180^\circ\times30^\circ$ view in azimuth and
elevation over the \SD array. \cref{fig:auger-map} shows their location. 
Starting anticlockwise with the telescopes located closest to Malargüe, these 
sites are \LL, \LM, \LA, \CO. Three additional telescope comprising the \HEATs
are located close to the \CO site.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*)]
  \item \textbf{The shutter}, which consists of two massive metal doors. It 
  shields the aperture from atmospheric influences such as strong winds or 
  rain. It also serves as a light shield that protects the sensitive camera 
  electronics during the day.

  \item A \textbf{\UV Filter} is located behind the shutters and blocks visible
  light from entering the camera. It separates the climate controlled inside of
  the \FD buildings from the outside via a circular aperture that measures 
  \SI{2.2}{\meter} in diameter. 
  
  \item \textbf{Corrector lenses} are located in a ring around the edge of the 
  aperture. The ring has an inside diameter of \SI{1.7}{\meter} and manages to 
  increase the light collection area, while minimizing spherical aberrations in
  the camera.

  \item \textbf{The curtain} provides a secondary (the shutter being primary) 
  failsafe that prevents high-intensity light from reaching the sensitive 
  electronics. Normally, the curtain sits outside of the light path, but can be
  deployed in emergency situations, or during daytime work and maintenance.

  \item \textbf{The mirrors} are a set of 60 hexagonal glass segments (36 
  square segments from anodized aluminum in the case of \LL and \LM) aligned 
  in a concave $\SI{3.6}{\meter}\times\SI{3.6}{\meter}$ shape. They reflect
  and concentrates incoming light towards

  \item \textbf{the Camera}, which is described in \cref{ssec:fd-camera}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{High Elevation Auger Telescope}
\label{sssec:HEAT}

The fifth and newest \FD-site, the \acl{HEAT} commissioned 2009, is located 
close to \CO, and looks out over the SD-750 array with three fluorescence 
telescopes. The setup of the optical system is conceptually identical to that
of the standard \FD-sites. However, in the case of \HEAT, the telescope 
buildings can be rotated upwards by $30^\circ$. This allows the observation of 
the atmosphere at higher elevations, where lower energy \EASs are more 
prevalent (Ref.\ \cref{sssec:heitler-matthews-model}). \HEAT thus extends the 
\FD energy scale downward by one order of magnitude 
\cite{mathesHEATTelescopesPierre2011}.

\subsection{Readout Electronics}
\label{ssec:fd-camera}

The camera for each telescope consists of 440 Photonis XP3062 \PMTs that serve 
as the individual pixels. The specific model is chosen because it minimizes 
afterpulsing in the anode trace, and has a reasonable quantum efficiency 
($\approx30\%$) at the target wavelength (\SIrange{350}{400}{\nano\meter}) that 
the \FD monitors \cite{xiaoCalibrationPhotonisXP30622005}. The \PMTs are 
arranged in a hexagonal grid with 22 rows and 20 columns (Ref.\ 
\cref{fig:fd-inside-view} and \cref{fig:cal-a-example-response}). Each pixel 
has a solid angle acceptance of $\Omega = \SI{5.94e-4}{\sr}$. Reflective star 
shapes with threefold symmetry sit between neighbouring pixels to boost the 
light collection efficiency, as well as increase the camera point resolution. 
The voltage at the anode of the \PMTs is read out at a sampling frequency of 
\SI{10}{\mega\hertz} (\SI{20}{\mega\hertz} for \HEAT) and digitized via 12-bit 
\ADC-converter stages. 

\subsection{Telescope Triggers}
\label{ssec:fd-triggers}

The accurate reconstruction of an \EAS requires the \FD-cameras to have an 
excellent background rejection (of e.g.\ transient stars in the camera \FOV). 
On top of this, the hybrid operation of the \FD detector requires that signals 
must be processed quickly to keep a potential timing offset between \FD and \SD
minimal. The \FD electronics achieve this via a simple, hierarchical trigger 
logic. 

A \FPGA scans the incoming signal from one column of pixels in a \FLT-stage. It
calculates the running sum of the last 16 (baseline subtracted \footnote{The
baseline is calculated as the mean \ADC value over the last 65536 
($\cong\SI{6.554}{\milli\second}$) samples}) \ADC samples per pixel and 
compares the result to a reference threshold. If the sum exceeds the reference 
threshold a \FLT is issued and the pixel data is written to a \SRAM. 

This is done to achieve two things. First, the number of \FLTs over a period of
time is used to calculate an \FLT rate per pixel. The reference threshold is 
updated according to this trigger rate, such that the \FLT matches a 
\SI{100}{\hertz} trigger rate. Secondly, the number of simultaneous \FLTs per 
column, or the column multiplicity, is evaluated and passed on to the 
\SLT-stage.

The \SLT scans the active pixels (those that have raised a trigger in the last 
\SI{100}{\nano\second}) for spatial correlations. If a collection of at least 4
pixels are found whose layout corresponds to a set of predefined masks a \SLT 
is issued. These predefined masks aim to identify straight tracks in the camera
\FOV, and decrease the trigger rate from $\SI{100}{\hertz\per\mathrm{pixel}}$ 
in the \FLT to 
$\SIrange[range-units = single]{0.1}{10}{\hertz\per\mathrm{telescope}}$ in the 
\SLT. Finally, a \TLT separates true air shower events from noise with 
$\approx94\%$ accuracy by performing a data-based cut on the multiplicity of 
active pixels \cite{abrahamFluorescenceDetectorPierre2010}. This further 
reduces the trigger rate to $\SI{0.01}{\hertz\per\mathrm{telescope}}$. 

\subsection{Telescope Calibration}
\label{ssec:fd-calibration}

The description of the event reconstruction in \cref{sec:event-reconstruction}
will reveal that in order to estimate the primary particle energy, the number 
of photons incident on the \FD-camera need to be known. This poses a big 
challenge. 

While the camera signal response correlates with the number of photons observed
per \EAS, the correlation coefficient is completely determined by the gain of 
the telescope readout electronics, which need not be constant. In fact, the 
camera response to the same signal can change by up to $6\%$ within the 
timespan of a few hours under suboptimal environmental conditions 
\cite{filipJumpsXYCalibration2024}.

To account for this, the \PMTs need to be calibrated on a nightly basis. During
calibration the camera is exposed to a lightsource of known radiance, and the 
camera response (in units of \ADC) is logged. Tying both bits of information 
together, this allows the calculation of pixel calibration constants, 
$C^\mathrm{pixel}_\mathrm{abs.}$, in units of \SI{}{$\gamma$\per\ADC}. 
Intuitively, $C^\mathrm{pixel}_\mathrm{abs.}$ holds information about the 
photon count (of a reference wavelength) required to elicit a signal response 
of \SI{1}{\ADC} in a given pixel-\PMT. 

It is imperative that the $C^\mathrm{pixel}_\mathrm{abs.}$ are measured with 
high precision, as their uncertainties contribute linearly to the uncertainty 
of the reconstructed primary particle energy. Currently, the pixel calibration 
constants are measured with an uncertainty of approximately \SI{9}{\percent}
\cite{dawsonUpdateAugerEnergy13}. This represents the biggest contribution to 
the total uncertainty in the \PAO energy scale of \SI{10}{\percent} 
\cite{verziEnergyScalePierre2013}. In the following, several methods are 
discussed that all aim to probe the pixel calibration constants. A further 
examination of the best-performing method is done in \cref{sec:xy-scanner}.

\subsubsection{Atmospheric Laser Shots}

The \PAO operates two stationary atmospheric lasers, the \CLF, and the \XLF 
\cite{collaborationTechniquesMeasuringAerosol2013}. Their primary science case 
is monitoring atmospheric conditions in order to model the development of \EASs
accurately. The two instruments provide vertical laser beams with stable power,
that, due to their very narrow frequency spectrum, translate to a constant
output of photons. In theory, the number of photons that scatter off of air 
molecules and hit the \FD-cameras per laser pulse is calculable. Analyzing the
telescope response to these laser pulses allow for an absolute calibration of 
\FD-\PMTs. In practice this proves impractical however. The air conditions in 
the atmosphere are not known with sufficient precision. Moreover, not all
pixels in all telescopes see the laser beams, which drastically lowers the 
accuracy of the presented method.

A roving laser that addresses both mentioned problems is in development and 
will serve as a useful method to cross-calibrate the \FD-\PMTs in the future 
\cite{uzeiroskaAbsoluteEnergyCalibration2025}.

\subsubsection{Drone Based Calibration}

A remote-controlled drone equipped with a light source can be used to simulate 
a distant point source in the \FD-\FOV \cite{wernerDesignTestFlying2010, 
tomankovaOpticalPropertiesCalibration2016}. In principle the hardware can be
used to calibrate the individual pixels of a camera, as the source radiance is
known. In practice the drone based approach takes a long time to illuminate 
every pixel. Other methods are less time intensive and therefore preferred. 
Nevertheless, the drone proves useful in measuring the optical properties of 
the \FD-mirrors, such as the \PSF \cite{ungerSimulationPointSpread2013}.

\subsubsection{Drum Calibration}
The absolute calibration method currently in use at the \PAO is the drum 
calibration. The name derives from the shape of the light source, visible in 
\cref{fig:drum-mounting}. It is a cylinder with a diameter of \SI{2.5}{\meter} 
and depth of \SI{1.4}{\meter} with two NSHU550 \UV \LEDs installed at its' 
center. A reflective laminate uniformly distributes the light from the \LED 
over the interior of the drum \cite{brackAbsolutePhotometricCalibration2004}. 
The radiance of the lightsource is carefully monitored during every \DAQ 
campaign \cite{brackAugerFluorescenceDetector2013}. 

The drum lightsource is mounted on top of the \FD aperture during calibration 
measurements. Once done, all pixels in the telescope camera are illuminated 
simultaneously with a single \LED pulse and the signal response can be read 
out.

While the measurement itself is quick it requires time, manpower, and knowledge
to set up. For this reason the last absolute calibration of the \FD-telescopes 
with the drum lightsource has been conducted more than ten years ago in 2013 
\cite{dorofeevFDAbsoluteCalibration2013}. 

\subsubsection{XY-Scanner}
\label{sssec:xy-scanner}

The XY-Scanner is a novel end-to-end calibration method that aims to be the
successor of the drum calibration system 
\cite{engelNewEndtoendCalibration2017}. Contrary to the drum, it consists of a
small, portable \IS lightsource mounted on an XY translation stage that is 
controlled via two stepper motors.

Instead of illuminating the entire aperture with a single flash of a big 
lightsource, the XY-Scanner fires many flashes from different positions in the 
aperture. This compromises measurement time for much easier handling and better
systematics on the resulting pixel calibration constants. The setup and 
analysis of the method are summarized in \cref{sec:xy-scanner}, and
\cite{schaferXYScannerVersatileMethod2023}

\subsubsection{Calibration A, B and C}
\label{sssec:relative-calibration}

In order to address the lack of a nightly absolute calibration relative 
calibration systems exists to track the telescope performance over time. These 
relative calibration systems are a set of \LCUs and identical for each 
building. Optical fibers transport photon pulses from the \LCU to diffusers 
located at three different exit ports in each of the telescopes per \FD site 
\cite{robertsCalibrationPierreAuger2003}. The location of the exit ports give 
raise to three different relative calibration methods:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*)]
	\item In the center of the mirror, directly illuminating the 
	\FD-camera.
	\item Above the camera body, illuminating the pixels indirectly via the
	mirror.
	\item Outside the \FD building and pointed towards the shutter.
	Reflective liners on the inside of the shutter redirect the light into 
	the aperture.
\end{enumerate}

In principle, the combination of Calibration A, B, and C allow to monitor the 
aging of the different detector components, and provide an end-to-end relative
calibration, in which the whole optical system of the telescopes is probed. 
Only Calibration A is performed to track changes in the pixel-\PMTs in 
practice. The  remaining telescope constituents are assumed to be stable
\cite{brackAugerFluorescenceDetector2013, dorofeevFDAbsoluteCalibration2013}. 
Results presented in this work call this assumption into question (Ref.\ 
\cref{sec:telescope-aging}).

Two Calibration A measurements are performed during each night. One before the 
start of \DAQ, and one after the data-taking ends. During the measurement the 
\LED emits 50 light pulses with a duration of $\sim60\,\upmu\mathrm{s}$ at a 
wavelength of \SI{470\pm25}{\nano\meter} \cite{menshikovLEDCalibrationUnit}. 
The signal is guided to the diffusers at the center of the mirrors. A seventh 
fiber feeds the light back to the \LCU, where a photodiode digitizes 
$S_\mathrm{LCU}$. 

The readout of pixel-data is forced via an external trigger coincident with 
every light pulse. The overall signal recorded in the \FD time trace from the 
calibration light pulse(s), $S_\mathrm{CalA}$, can be recovered by first 
estimating and subtracting the trace baseline 
\cite{schaferReconstructionBaselineUndershoot2022}, determining the pulse start
and stop via a pulse-finding algorithm (Ref.\ 
\cref{sssec:reconstruction-pulse-finder}), and summing the \ADC bins within 
the pulse-window. \cref{fig:cal-a-example-response} shows the signal of an 
example Cal.\ A measurement analyzed in this fashion. From $S_\mathrm{LCU}$ and
$S_\mathrm{CalA}$ the calibration constants measured in a reference night via 
calibration method $i$ can be forward- or backpropagated to an arbitrary point 
in time according to \cite{salinaNewCalibrationTask2014, 
salinaAnalisiDatiXY2025} like

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cal-a-propagation}
C^{\mathrm{pixel},\,i}_{\mathrm{abs.}} = C^{\mathrm{pixel},\,i}_{\mathrm{abs.},\,\mathrm{ref.}}\,\cdot\,\frac{S_\mathrm{LCU}}{S_\mathrm{LCU}^\mathrm{ref.}}\,\frac{S_\mathrm{CalA}^\mathrm{ref.}}{S_\mathrm{CalA}}\,c_\mathrm{LCU}^\mathrm{Corr.}\,c^\mathrm{Corr.}_\mathrm{Halo}.
\end{equation}

In \cref{eq:cal-a-propagation}, a time-dependant factor 
$c^\mathrm{Corr.}_\mathrm{LCU}$ absorbs the change of radiance in the \LCU 
after the \LED was changed in all telescopes in 2011/2012 
\cite{menshikovLEDCalibrationUnit}. The second factor, 
$c^\mathrm{Corr.}_\mathrm{Halo}$, corrects a loss of signal due to reflections 
on the camera body \cite{brackFluorescenceDetectorAbsolute13}.

\section{The Surface Detector}
\label{sec:sd}

The \acf{SD} is a collection of more than 1660 stations spread in a regular 
pattern over the surface of the Pampa Amarilla. The position of each station is
shown in \cref{fig:auger-map}. Each station is equipped with hardware that 
offers several detection channels to ionizing particles on the ground. The
remote location of each station complicates maintenance work and the transfer 
of measurement data. For this reason the stations are operating largely 
autonomously, only communicating essential information to the \CDAS. They 
derive their electrical power from a solar panel, and have (limited) 
computational resources to manage software control flow.  

\subsection{Array Structure}
\label{ssec:array-structure}

The \SD is designed to detect the footprint of an \EAS on the surface of the 
Earth. The choice of a detector segmented into many individual stations is
deliberate. Operating a detector with a continous active region is impractical,
as \EASs can reach many kilometers in extent. It is sufficient to probe the 
shower footprint at select few positions and reconstruct the \EAS from this 
limited knowledge. As already realized by Pierre Auger, the separation between 
sampling points influences the types of air showers that can be detected 
\cite{augerExtensiveCosmicRayShowers1939}. The finer the sampling, the more 
low-energy \EAS become observable.

Already before the construction of the \PAO it was decided that the \SD will 
host sub-arrays of different sizes and separations 
\cite{watsonDevelopmentPierreAuger}. This ensures that the observatory can 
make accurate measurements of \EAS over many decades of energy. 
\cref{tab:sub-array-details} lists the most important characteristics of the 
individual sub-arrays. In all of them, the stations are arranged in a grid of 
isoceles triangles. 

The SD-1500, or main array, is the largest of the three existing grids. The 
number represents the side length of \SI{1500}{\meter} of the triangular grid.
It is to date the detector with the highest accumulated exposure to \CRs 
worldwide \cite{aabPierreAugerObservatory2020}.

Inset in the north-west of the SD-1500 are the SD-750 (Infill), and SD-433 as
can be seen in \cref{fig:auger-map}. Their vicinity to \CO and \HEAT ensure 
that the hybrid reconstruction of \EAS remain possible for energies below 
\SI{1e17}{\eV} \cite{mathesHEATTelescopesPierre2011}. Contained within both 
arrays are several \RND environments, such as the \AERA 
\cite{fuchsAugerEngineeringRadio2012}.

The Infill is also home to doublet and triplet detectors, where two or more 
\SD-stations are installed in close proximity to each other. These setups can
be used to study timing offsets and (un)certainties in the signal readout chain
of the \UUB (Ref.\ \cref{ssec:uub}) electronics 
\cite{payerasCompatibilityUBUUB2023}.

\subsubsection{Water Cherenkov Detector}
\label{sssec:wcd}

The \acf{WCD} is the main detector component, and common to all \SD-stations 
since the construction of the \PAO began. It is an oblate cylinder with a 
diameter of \SI{3.6}{\meter} and height of $\SI{1.2}{\meter}$ as seen in 
\cref{fig:sd-station-render}. The interior is filled with a sealed, reflective 
liner containing \SI{12000}{\liter} of filtered water, and shielded from 
exterior light via a polyethylene housing 
\cite{allekotteSurfaceDetectorSystem2008}. 

The darkness in the cylinder is occasionally illuminated by flashes of light 
stemming from relativistic particles producing Cherenkov radiation in the 
water. The Cherenkov light scatters off the interior walls until it reaches 
one of three identical \SI{230}{\milli\meter} Photonis XP1805 \LPMTs 
\cite{tripathiSystematicStudyLarge2003} that are installed at the top of the 
polyethylene tank, and that are optically coupled to the inside volume of the 
liner.

The \WCD needs to be able to accurately reconstruct a large dynamic range of
signals, starting from individual particles to $>10^3$ muons entering the water
\cite{abrahamTriggerApertureSurface2010}. To achieve this, the \LPMT signals 
are read out in two channels, the \HG channel and the \LG channel. The \LG 
is connected directly to the dynode of the \PMTs, whereas the \HG tracks the 
anode voltage. The \HG-channel is amplified to have a gain ratio of $\sim32$. 
Both channels are read out via 12-bit AD9628 \FADCs 
\cite{analogdevicesinc.AD9628DatasheetProduct2015} at a sampling rate of 
\SI{120}{\mega\hertz}, corresponding to a binning of \SI{8.333}{\nano\second}.

A fourth, \SI{25}{\milli\meter} \SPMT of type Hamamatsu R8619 
\cite{hamamatsuphotonicsPhotomultiplierTubeR8619} is installed near the center
of the tank. Less light enters the \SPMT due to its smaller faceplate. It can 
therefore handle higher particle densities before saturation. As such, the 
\SPMT extends the dynamic range of the \WCD to $\sim20\,000$ particles 
\cite{collaborationPierreAugerObservatory2016}.

Just like for the \FD the detector, data from the \WCD needs to be put into 
a physical context in order to be interpreted correctly. For the particle 
detectors at the \PAO this context is given by atmospheric muons, as they have 
a characteristic energy deposit and signature in the active medium. 

The \WCD  measurement data is converted from units of \ADC to the quantity of 
muons that would need to pass vertically through the center of the water tank 
in order to record a signal of equal strenght. This gives raise to two 
quantities that need to be tracked in order to provide calibrated data:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \Ivem is the most probable pulse height in units of \ADC 
	recorded by the \WCD \PMTs when a muon passes vertically through the 
	center of the water volume.
	\item \Qvem gives the most probable value for the sum of all \ADC 
	samples (i.e.\ the integral) of the muon pulse, corresponding to its 
	deposited energy.
\end{itemize}

The trigger thresholds (Ref.\ \cref{ssec:sd-triggers}) in the \WCD are set in
terms of \Ivem. For this reason, the stations operate a self-calibration
algorithm that delivers a rate-based estimate of \Ivem, called \VemOnline that
is used to update trigger thresholds continuously. \VemOnline is accurate to 
$\sim5-10\%$ of the true value of \Ivem 
\cite{bertouCalibrationSurfaceArray2006}.

\VemOnline is adjusted by a simple and robust algorithm. It employs a 
calibration trigger that requires a coincident signal of at least 
$1.75\,\VemOnline$ in all three \LPMTs. In the case of one or more \PMTs
being non-functional the trigger thresholds are adjusted in order to ensure 
the stable operation of the remaining \PMTs 
\cite{convengaLocalStationCalibrationDummies2023}.

An additional cut of $2.5\,\VemOnline$ is applied per \LPMT, and the number 
of triggers during a timespan measuring at most \SI{60}{\second} is tracked. If
the trigger rate of the calibration trigger exceeds a target rate of 
\SI{72}{\hertz} during this period, the value of \VemOnline is increased. This 
results in more stringent trigger conditions, and will decrease the trigger 
rate during the following \DAQ period. Similarly, the value of \VemOnline is 
decreased if the calibration trigger rate is lower than \SI{68}{\hertz}. If the
trigger rate lies within \SI{70\pm2}{\hertz} the \DAQ period is increased 
(beginning with \SI{10}{\second}) by \SI{5}{\second} to limit the influence of 
Poissonian noise on the final estimate \cite{bertouCalibrationSurfaceArray2006,
convengaLocalStationCalibrationDummies2023}.

Under nominal conditions, this results in the value of \VemOnline being updated
every minute. Most importantly, this implies that the trigger thresholds of 
station-level event triggers adjust to the changing environmental conditions in
the array. 

Additionally, the value of \VemOnline is packaged among other diagnostic 
information, and sent to the \CDAS every \SI{400}{\second} for monitoring 
purposes. This allows for the easy identification of non-functioning stations, 
as they will show an abnormal value or evolution of \VemOnline in one or more 
of the three \LPMTs. 

An alternative method that is used during event reconstruction (i.e. not on a 
station-level for trigger/monitoring purposes) achieves a $2\%$ uncertainty on 
the final estimator. It is described in more detail in 
\cref{sssec:reconstruction-histogram-fitter}.

\subsubsection{Surface Scintillator Detector}
\label{sssec:ssd}

With the AugerPrime detector upgrade, the \acf{SSD} has been installed on every
\SD-station. It sits atop the \WCD as seen in \cref{fig:sd-station-render}, 
with an aluminum box shielding the sensitive electronics from the environment. 
As the name implies, the active area of the detector 
(\SI{3.84}{\meter\squared}) is made up of polystyrene scintillator strips 
measuring \SI{160}{\centi\meter} in length, \SI{5}{\centi\meter} in width, and
\SI{1}{\centi\meter} in thickness \cite{cataldiUpgradePierreAuger2022}. These 
strips produce light via molecular (de-) excitation when ionizing particles 
pass through them, with the amount of light being inversely proportional to the
change in energy of the traversing particles.

The scintillating light is reflected by a TiO$_2$ external coating of the bars,
and eventually captured by \acf{WLS} cables running through the long end of 
each segment. The inner workings of the \SSD are displayed in 
\cref{fig:ssd-high-contrast}. From there, the \WLSs are arranged in a "U" 
configuration, where both ends of each cable are coupled via optical cement to 
a \SI{38}{\milli\meter} Hamamatsu R942 \PMT 
\cite{hamamatsuphotonicsPhotomultiplierTubeR9420}. This allows the efficient 
transport of photons from the source to the readout location. The electronics 
responsible for digitizing the signal of the \SSD \PMT have similar 
specifications to those of the \WCD 
\cite{collaborationPierreAugerObservatory2016}.

A crucial difference between the \WCD and the \SSD is though their response 
to the \EM-component of \EASs. While the \WCD is mostly sensitive to muons, the
\SSD, relying only on ionization processes, shows the same signal strength for
muons and electrons. Simultaneous analysis of the \WCD and \SSD allows the 
disentanglement of the individual shower components. This results in an 
improved sensitivity to the primary particle mass (Ref.\ 
\cref{ssec:different-primaries} and \cref{ssec:augerprime-and-outlook}). 

Like the \WCD, the \SSD data must be calibrated. Due to the different response 
the calibration is derived from an arbitrary \acf{MIP}:\footnote{As 
opposed to a muon in the case of the \WCD}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \Imip is the most probable pulse height in units of \ADC recorded
	by the \SSD \PMT when a \MIP passes vertically through a random 
	location in the \SSD.
	\item \Qmip is the most probable pulse integral recorded during the 
	event.
\end{itemize}

The \SSD data are calibrated during event reconstruction according to the 
process outlined in \cref{sssec:reconstruction-histogram-fitter}. Additionally,
a novel algorithm developed in this work allows the estimation of \Imip on a 
station-level. Implementation details can be found in \todo{ref here}.

\label{sssec:umd}

\begin{figure}[t]

  \label{fig:umd}
  }\hspace{0.2cm}
  \subfloat[]{\includegraphics[height=0.33\textwidth]{auger-observatory/uub_annotated.png}
  \label{fig:uub}
  }
  \caption[]{\subref{fig:umd}The three modules of a \UMD in their final 
  location before being covered with soil. Additional solar panels in the 
  background provide electrical power for the detector. From 
  \cite{pierreaugercollaborationUMDUndergroundMuon} \subref{fig:uub} The \PCB
  design of the \UUB. Connection with the front-end electronics is made via the
  golden connectors on the bottom. Image adopted with changes from

  \label{fig:umd-and-uub}

The \acf{UMD} represents another enhancement installed during the AugerPrime 
detector upgrade. It boosts the observatories primary mass discrimination 
abilities \cite{collaborationPierreAugerObservatory2016}. In total 73 \UMDs are buried underground, each one in close vicinity to an SD-750 
or SD-433 station. These installations comprise the \acf{AMIGA} detector.

Each \UMD consists of three \SI{10.5}{\meter\squared} scintillator modules that
are, like the \SSD, segmented themselves into bars to minimize signal pileup
\cite{bottiStatusPerformanceUnderground2022}. They are buried \SI{2.3}{\meter} 
underground, as shown in \cref{fig:umd}. The soil above the detectors provides 
an excellent attenuation for non-muonic particles in \EASs. Particularly, this 
implies that the signals measured by the \UMD consist entirely of muons, 
allowing the \PAO to e.g.\ probe the muon content of \EASs 
\cite{jesusStudyMassComposition2025} more effectively.

\WLS cables transport the light signal from the active medium of each bar to a 
dedicated \SiPM. 64 \SiPMs for the 64 bars in each module are bundled together.
The output of the \SiPM arrays is digitized in two ways 
\cite{aabCalibrationUndergroundMuon2021}:

\begin{itemize}
	\item 64 binary traces, that read either 1, or 0, depending on if the 
	corresponding \SiPM signal is above or below a threshold of 
	\SI{2.5}{photo-equivalents}\footnote{The photo-equivalent gives the 
	number of illuminated photodiodes in the \SiPM (1584 in total).} 
	\cite{bottiStatusPerformanceUnderground2022}.
	\item one \ADC trace that sums all 64 \SiPM signals. Split into \HG and
	\LG channels.
\end{itemize}

This approach is chosen to retain the large dynamic range of signals that the 
detector needs to support. Individual muons can be reconstructed by examining
the binary mode of the \UMD, and a muon density of up to 
$\sim\SI{30}{muons\per\meter\squared}$ during a high-energy \EAS event can be 
constructed from the so-called \ADC mode 
\cite{bottiStatusPerformanceUnderground2022}.

The muon density is recovered by dividing the observed pulse integral, or 
charge, by the most probable charge obtained from a single-muon event, 
$Q_\mathrm{UMD}$. 

This is a familiar problem, and has been solved for the \WCD already (Ref.\ 
\cref{sssec:wcd}). A similar strategy for the calibration of the \UMD data is 
thus chosen. The \UMD does not need to be self-calibrating, as it is run purely
in a secondary mode to the \WCD, and does not generate triggers itself 
\cite{aabCalibrationUndergroundMuon2021}.

\subsubsection{Radio Detector}
\label{sssec:rd}

The \RD represents the last detector component that was added to the \SD. Since
the completion of construction at the end of 2024 
\cite{castellinaSeasonalGreetings202424}, all \SD stations have been equipped
with \SALLAs, consisting of a dipole loop with radius \SI{60}{\centi\meter} 
that are mounted on top of the \SSD (Ref.\ \cref{fig:sd-station-render}). 

This configuration allows the detection of the \EM signature of \EASs from 
arbitrary directions in the frequency band from
\SIrange[range-units = single]{30}{80}{\mega\hertz} 
\cite{pontLargeRadioDetector2021}, and was chosen after a thorough \RND phase 
of the \AERA prototype detector \cite{aabPierreAugerObservatory2020, 
fuchsAugerEngineeringRadio2012, aabEnergyEstimationCosmic2016}.

The \RD is only sensitive to macroscopic changes in the \EM field, and cannot 
rely on muonic signals for calibration. For this reason, the \RD too is not
self-calibrating. \RD data are put into a physical context only during an \EAS
event reconstruction. Then, the signal power received by the antenna is 
compared to the time- and frequency-dependent expected signal of the galactic 
radio background. This provides a measure of the antenna directional gain that 
agrees to within $\sim5\%$ with simulations \cite{huegeRadioDetectorPierre2023,
fodranFirstResultsAugerPrime2022}.

\subsection{Station Electronics}
\label{ssec:uub}

The \acf{UUB}, replacing the \UB in the AugerPrime detector upgrade, hosts all 
main electronic readout components of the station 
\cite{collaborationPierreAugerObservatory2016}. \cref{fig:uub} and 
\cref{fig:uub-circuit-diagram} show the design and architecture of the \UUB 
\acf{PCB} respectively, and \cite{suomijarviNewElectronicsSurface2018} details
the exact implementation and purpose of each component on the stations 
motherboard. 

Highlighted for this work are the operative functions of the Artix-7 \FPGA 
\cite{xilinx7SeriesFPGAs2020}, which is responsible for low-level signal 
processing, as well as the tasks of the Xilinx Zynq-7020 \SOC, equipped with a
dual-core CPU \cite{amdZynq7000SoC2023} that handles higher-level programming 
logic. 

\subsubsection{FPGA and Memory Buffers}
\label{sssec:fpga-buffers}

The main purpose of the \FPGA is to read out the 10 different channels 
($\SI{5}{\PMTs} \times (\HG + \LG)$) of detector data and compare them against 
various trigger criteria \cite{nitzUUBV2Analog18}. If a trigger condition is 
met, the \FPGA stores the \HG and \LG data in memory for further processing. 
The memory address is determined by the type of raised trigger. Four \FIFO ring
buffers are available for writing and separated in their purpose. Two instances
of the circular memory modules comprise the muon buffer, and two form the 
shower buffer. 

The former contains events that surpass $30\,\ADC$ ($70\,\ADC$) above baseline 
in any of the \WCD \LPMTs (\SSD PMT) for at least one sample. Once such a pulse
level is observed, the last 19 bins, the trigger latch bin, as well as the next
49 bins (concluding in total 69 bins, or $\sim0.575\,\upmu\mathrm{s}$) from the
\WCD and \SSD \HG channels are stored.
This process occurs at a rate of roughly $\SI{3}{\kilo\Hz}$, with the \FPGA 
alternating writing between both ring buffers to avoid dead times.

The shower buffer contains events that satisfy \TTWOs (Ref.\ 
\cref{ssec:sd-triggers}). These occur at a, due to the stations 
self-calibration, fixed rate of $\sim\SI{20}{\hertz}$. While this is a far 
lower rate than that of the muon buffer, the volume of information contained in
the shower buffer is more exhaustive. Upon a \TTWO, 2048 samples 
($\sim17.07\,\upmu\mathrm{s}$) of all ten \HG and \LG channels are stored 
in the circular buffer. This data are the raw event information that gets sent 
to the \CDAS upon an \TTHREE request (Ref.\ \cref{ssec:cdas}). In total, the 
two shower buffers can hold up to 1024 \TTWO events 
\cite{wainbergDigitalElectronicsPierre2014}, meaning that at a nominal \TTWO
rate, events are overwritten after roughly $\SI{50}{\second}$. In practice, not
more than $\SI{8}{\second}$ are needed for the \CDAS to request \TTWO 
information\footnote{Thunderstorms can drastically increase this time. For 
this, a partial fix was presented in \todo[inline]{cite icrc2025 proceeding}.}
\cite{collaborationPierreAugerObservatory2016}.

Since muons in \EASs are produced in the minimum of the Bethe-Bloch 
curve, they have a characteristic energy deposit and detector signature in the 
\WCD and \SSD. In order to use this information for calibration purposes the 
CPU analyzes all $n$ traces (containing 69 samples $b_i$ of 
data) collected in the muon buffers (Ref.\ \cref{sssec:fpga-buffers}) and 
calculates their baseline $B$, peaks $I_n$, and charges $Q_n$ per trace as 

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:peak-and-charge}
B = \frac{1}{n} \sum^n_{i=1} b_{0,\,i}\,, \qquad I_n = \mathrm{max}\left( b_{n} - B_\mathrm{prev.} \right), \qquad Q_n = \sum^{69}_{j=1} b_{j,\,n} - B_\mathrm{prev.}. 
\end{equation}

$B_\mathrm{prev.}$ in \cref{eq:peak-and-charge} gives the value of the first 
bin averaged over all traces of the previously analyzed \SI{61}{\second} 
interval \cite{satoUUBDAQV128r0b0p292024}. The distributions of $I_n$ and $Q_n$
are logged in histograms with predefined binning 
\cite{bertouCalibrationSurfaceArray2006}, one example is shown in 
\cref{fig:ssd-histo}. This process is repeated every \SI{61}{\second} for all 
\PMTs. 

\subsection{Station Triggers}
\label{ssec:sd-triggers}

The autonomy of the \SD-stations requires them to independently recognize when
an \EAS passes through them. Several algorithms are in place to detect detector
data that deviates from the electronic noise, or low-threshold pulses stemming 
from e.g.\ atmospheric muons. If one of these algorithms observes a noteworthy event
the local station sends a corresponding \acf{T2} to the \CDAS. Importantly, 
this does not imply the detection of an \EAS, but just an \EAS candidate. The 
\acfp{T3} are discussed in \cref{ssec:cdas}. 

\subsubsection{Compatibility Mode}

All station level trigger algorithms presented hereafter have been in operation
since many years, with the majority being present since the construction of the
first \WCD tanks \cite{thepierreaugercollaborationPierreAugerObservatory1997}. 
This level of maturity also means that the various trigger hyperparameters are
optimized for \UB electronics, which have a \SI{40}{\mega\hertz} sampling rate
(\SI{25}{\nano\second} binning), compared to what is in use today in the \UUB 
(\SI{8.333}{\nano\second} binning). 

To address this, the same trigger algorithms that were in use for the \UB are 
operated with the same hyperparameters in a compatibility mode, where incoming 
\UUB detector data is \acf{FDS} to resemble \UB data. More specifically, a 
\FDS fake \UB trace $\hat{f}$ (containing 682 samples) is calculated from a 
weighted sum of the \acf{FBW} trace $f$ (2048 samples) like  
\cite{convengaLocalStationCalibrationDummies2023}:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:filter-and-downsample} 
\hat{f}_i = \frac{1}{2048} \sum^{2 n}_{j = 0} f_{3i+p-j} \, \mathrm{FIR}_{n - \left| j-n \right|}, \qquad p = \left\{ 0, 1, 2 \right\}.
\end{equation}

In \cref{eq:filter-and-downsample}, $p$ gives the phase offset for the 
downsampling of the filtered trace, which is quasi-random, and based on the 
uptime of the \FPGA.

The \FIR weights applied to the \FBW trace are given as
$\mathrm{FIR} = \left\{ 5, 0, 12, 22, 0, -61, -96, 0, 256, 551, 681 \right\}$, 
and $n=10$. The normalizing factor $2048$ differs slightly from 
$\sum^{2n}_{j=0} \mathrm{FIR}_{n-\left| j-n \right|} = 2059$, and is chosen for
simplicity because the division by $2048 = 2^{11}$ can be realized by a 11-bit 
shift to the right. A comparison between a \FBW and \FDS trace of the same 
signal is shown in \cref{fig:filter-and-downsample}.

\subsubsection{Threshold Trigger}
\label{sssec:threshold-t2}

The simplest form of a station level trigger is a \SB threshold algorithm, that
triggers a \TTWO whenever the \HG channels of all \WCD \LPMTs simulatenously 
exceed the equivalent of \SI{3.2}{\VemOnline} (\SI{3.6}{\VemOnline}/
\SI{5.0}{\VemOnline}) in \ADC counts above baseline in the case of all (2/1) 
working \LPMTs. The chosen thresholds stabilize the \TTWO rate generated by 
this \SB trigger to $\sim\SI{20}{\hertz}$ 
\cite{abrahamTriggerApertureSurface2010}.

This trigger rate is much higher than the rate of \TTHREE triggers (i.e.\ true
\EAS events), which measures $\sim\SI{2}{\per\minute}$ for the entire \SD 
\cite{ramellaT3RateCompatibility2025}. The threshold trigger thus has a very 
low purity. This is known, but must be accepted to retain event detection 
efficiency for inclined showers that might be induced by neutrinos 
\cite{anchordoquiExoticNeutrinoInteractions2006}.

\subsubsection{Time Over Threshold}

The \ToT trigger is an altered version of the threshold trigger. Instead of 
requiring a \SB exceedance of a high threshold, the \ToT scans a window of 120
samples of the \FDS traces for the repeated crossing of a lower threshold.

The threshold is defined as \SI{0.2}{\VemOnline}, and a \TTWO is raised if two 
or more \PMTs have a count of $>12$ out of a 120-bin scan window above 
threshold.

Applying a consecutivity requirement not only to the different \PMTs, but also 
to the signal over time presents a simple change to the \SB threshold trigger, 
but drastically decreases the trigger frequency to $\SI{2}{\hertz}$ 
\cite{ramellaT3RateCompatibility2025}, and improves its purity.

\subsubsection{Time Over Threshold Deconvoluted}

The \ToTd trigger has the same trigger requirements as the \ToT, with the only
difference being the input data that is fed into the trigger. Before passing 
the \ToTd, the filtered and downsampled detector data is convoluted with a 
kernel that accounts for the exponential decay of light intensity from a single
flash.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:totd-input}
\hat{f}^*_i = \left( \hat{f}_i - \hat{f}_{i-1} \, e^{-\SI{25}{\nano\second}/\SI{67}{\nano\second}} \right)\,/\,\left( 1 - e^{-\SI{25}{\nano\second}/\SI{67}{\nano\second} } \right).
\end{equation}

\cite{billoirPeakSearchingSurface2002, colemanNewTriggerSettings2018} go into 
further detail and motivates the \SI{25}{\nano\second} in \cref{eq:totd-input} 
as the binning of the \UB, and \SI{67}{\nano\second} is the time constant of 
the exponential decay, as determined by measurements. This reduces the 
exponential tail of the \WCD signal to a series of (in theory) \SB pulses whose
pulse height correlates well with the deposited total signal. The \ToTd has 
been in use in the \SD since 2013. It extends the sensitivity of the \SD 
stations to lower \EAS primary energies \cite{billoirFirstResultsToTd2013}.

\subsubsection{Multiplicity Of Positive Steps}

The \MoPS trigger is the last and most intricate addition to \SD \TTWO triggers
\cite{billoirPropositionImproveLocal13}. Instead of relying on the \WCD signal
amplitude and accurate \PMT calibration, the \MoPS trigger is sensitive to the 
signal shape. 

Specifically, it scans the \LPMT traces for rising flanks, where consecutive 
bins show an increase in \ADC counts. After applying several selection criteria
on the magnitude of the rising flank (Ref.\ 
\cite{colemanNewTriggerSettings2018}), the number of detected positive steps in
a window of 120 bins is counted. If the number of rising flanks exceeds four 
for at least two \LPMTs, a \TTWO is sent to the \CDAS.


\section{Event Reconstruction}
\label{sec:event-reconstruction}

\halflift

\subsection{Event Detection}
\label{ssec:cdas}

The first action in the chain of \EAS event reconstruction is the detection of
an air shower. Based on the hybridity of the observatory, this happens in a 
twofold way. Common to either case is the principle of a hierarchical trigger
structure, where detector information is processed in-situ where possible, and 
forwarded only if certain (trigger) conditions are met. The identification of
an \EAS event at the \PAO starts with an \acf{T3}, that occurs at the level of
the \CDAS.

\subsubsection{Fluorescence Detector}

The lower level triggers analyzing camera data were introduced in 
\cref{ssec:fd-triggers} and are split into \FLT, \SLT, and \acf{TLT}. \TLTs 
from all telescopes in a building are analyzed for events that pass the \FOV of
multiple cameras/telescopes, and merged if this is the case. The merged \TLTs 
are promoted to \FD-\TTHREEs thereafter.

The \CDAS scans the collection of \FD-\TTHREEs and \SD-\TTHREEs (Ref.\ next 
section) for temporal coincidences in order to identify hybrid events. If no
corresponding \SD-\TTHREE is recognized for a given \FD-\TTHREE, a preliminary
analysis of telescope data gives the approximate impact time of the \EAS on 
ground. The \SD-stations closest to the participating \FD sites are checked for
\TTWOs that lie within $20\,\upmu\mathrm{s}$ of this core time. All stations 
with a matching trigger, are added to the \FD-event 
\cite{abrahamFluorescenceDetectorPierre2010, 
thepierreaugercollaborationPierreAugerCosmic2015}.

\subsubsection{Surface Detector}

For the \SD, the trigger hierarchy is based on the \acfp{T2} detailed in 
\cref{ssec:sd-triggers}. Every station sends a list if its \TTWO candidate 
events to the \CDAS, along with a high-precision timestamp when the event 
occured. 

The \CDAS analzyes the spatial and temporal distribution of \TTWOs for
coincidences, as these may be caused by \EASs. For any \TTWO at a candidate 
station, the surrounding crowns $C_1, ..., C_4$ representing the sets of  
$1\mathrm{st}, ..., 4\mathrm{th}$ nearest neighbor stations are analyzed for 
coincident \TTWO triggers. A grace period of $(6 + 5n)\,\mathrm{ns}$ is allowed
for a station in $C_n$ to be coincident with the candidate station due to the 
finite propagation velocity of the shower front 
\cite{abrahamTriggerApertureSurface2010}. 

Two scanning strategies are utilized when searching for \acfp{T3}. Both rely on
a compactness criteria of the underlaying \TTWOs.

The 3-fold mode requires all participating station triggers to be \ToT-like, 
meaning \ToT, \ToTd, or \MoPS (Ref.\ \cref{ssec:sd-triggers} ) only are 
considered for the event formation. Then, at least two \ToT-like stations 
(including the central station $C_0$) are required within $C_1$, and at least 
three triggered stations must exist up to the $2\mathrm{nd}$ nearest neighbors
in $C_2$. \cref{fig:t3-vertical} gives an example pattern for a 3-fold \TTHREE.

An example of the second mode, the 4-fold \TTHREE trigger, is shown in 
\cref{fig:t3-horizontal}. It accepts any kind of \TTWO, namely the noisy 
threshold trigger (Ref.\ \cref{sssec:threshold-t2}). It requires four active 
stations. At least two stations (including the central station) in 
$C_1$, at least three within $C_2$, and one more trigger anywhere within $C_3$
or $C_4$.

Due to their respective selection criteria, both trigger configurations have 
their own use case. The 3-fold \TTHREE, due to the \ToT-like \TTWO triggers
providing an excellent background rejection, has a fraction of true \EAS events
that surpasses \SI{95}{\percent} \todo{ICRC proceeding}. It provides the main 
trigger mode for showers impinging on the array with an inclination of up 
to $\sim\SI{60}{\degree}$. Higher inclination showers are attenuated more 
strongly. Usually, the \EM component of horizontal \EASs gets thermalized 
before the shower reaches the ground, and only the muonic component survives. 
This results in short, strong pulses and threshold \TTWOs being recorded in the
local stations. The \SB \TTWO (and 4-fold \TTHREE by extension) thus represent 
the main trigger for highly inclined shower events, at the cost of a purity of 
only \SIrange{3}{10}{\percent} \todo{add proceeding} \cite{abrahamTriggerApertureSurface2010}.

\subsubsection{Event selection}

The purity of the \SD-\TTHREEs especially is poor. To combat this, fiducial 
triggers need to be employed before the event reconstruction can take place. 

Immediately after a \TTHREE is issued and low-level detector data has been sent
to the \CDAS, a T4 trigger rejects \TTHREE candidates whose participating 
station trigger timings do not fit a plane front hypothesis. Essentially, this 
identifies \TTHREEs that are formed on the basis of random coincidences of 
\TTWOs. Additionally, distinct event-level triggers coincident in time and 
space are merged into the same event. The ratio of real \EAS events over 
generated T4s is roughly \SIrange{98}{100}{\percent} \cite{abrahamTriggerApertureSurface2010}. \todo{cite icrc abstract}

A final T5 check requires the station that received the highest particle count
in the shower footprint (called the hottest station) is surrounded by 6 
($\geq5$) 
functioning nearest neighbour stations functioning for a 6T5 (5T5) trigger. 

While the requirement reduces the effective size of the \SD-array by about 
\SI{10}{\percent} \cite{abrahamTriggerApertureSurface2010}, it ensures the 
reconstructed quantities are not biased by the geometry of the array, which can
occur when an \EAS is detected e.g.\ at the edges of the \SD array. 

\subsection{\Offline Framework}

\Offline is the extensive in-house software framework utilized by the Auger 
collaboration to reconstruct \EAS data and perform detector studies
\cite{argiroOfflineSoftwareFramework2007}. It is written in \texttt{C++}
\cite{stroustrupStandard}, and is continuously improved upon by collaborators 
using \texttt{git} \cite{chaconProGit2014, 
thepierreaugercollaborationOfficialOfflineRepository2025,}. A deliberate design
choice of the programming environment is the compartmentalization of the 
reconstruction process into different modules.

During an \EAS reconstruction, these modules are executed in a predefined 
sequence as instructed by \XML steering files. Every module has a specific use 
case, and reconstructs different \EAS information. 

Consequentially, the module sequence defines which measurement data is 
analyzed, and which \EAS information is reconstructed. Usually, at the end of 
the procedure the results of the analysis are written to an \ADST, a compact 
data structure based on the \texttt{root} format 
\cite{brunROOTObjectOriented1997}. 

The goal of the event reconstruction is the collection of several quantities 
that can be used for higher-level physics analysis. An overview of the most 
important reconstructed information for the \FD and the \SD is given in 
\cref{tab:rec-quantities}. 

Note that for the \SD reconstruction the table contains only quantities 
pertaining to the \WCD and the \SSD, and \UMD. The \RD event reconstruction 
chain is, given the novelty of the detector, still subject to changes, and in 
any case not relevant in this work. \todo{simons proceeding} details the procedure to reconstruct \EAS observables from radio data. 

The following sections attempt to detail the different steps that are executed 
during a standard event reconstruction. Where possible, the exact module names 
(as implemented in Ref.\ 
\cite{thepierreaugercollaborationOfficialOfflineRepository2025}) are used as 
section headers. Wherever a secion header is marked with an asterisk, steps
from different modules haven been combined, or details in a single module have
been expanded in order to boost readability. At several occasions 
simplifications are made, or details have been left out. As such, the following
pages are meant to be understood as a surface level introduction to the 
\Offline event reconstruction, not an in-depth discussion of the algorithm.

\noindent The \Offline source code
\cite{thepierreaugercollaborationOfficialOfflineRepository2025} is taken as a 
reference for all following information.

\subsection{FD Reconstruction}
\label{ssec:fd-reconstruction}

\halflift

\subsubsection{FdCalibratorOG}
\label{sssec:reconstruction-pixel-calibrator}

First, timing corrections are applied to the \FD-\TTWO timestamps in order to 
synchronize measurement information across all telescopes that have observed 
the \EAS. 

For each pixel in each telescope the baseline is evaluated as the mean \ADC 
values of the first 300 samples in the corresponding \PMT trace. Furthermore, 
the pixel calibration constants that give the quantity of light that causes a 
specific \ADC detector response are collected from an external database. As 
outlined in \cref{ssec:fd-calibration}, two Calibration A measurements are 
performed each night, once before, and once after \DAQ. The pixel calibration
constants to which the detector data are normalized against stem from the 
post-\DAQ Calibration A of the respective night.

By subtracting the baseline $b$ and applying the current pixel calibration 
constants $C_\mathrm{abs.}^\mathrm{pixel}$ the uncalibrated trace 
$f^\mathrm{ADC}$ is converted from units of \ADC to a calibrated trace 
$f^\gamma$ that is given in numbers of photons at a reference wavelength.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fd-calib-equation}
f^\gamma = \left( f^\mathrm{ADC} - b \right)\,/\,C_\mathrm{abs.}^\mathrm{pixel}.
\end{equation}

Every \PMT is checked for an \ADC overflow. This occurs when the \ADC signal of
the pixel exceeds $2^{11} - 1 = 2047\,\ADC$\footnote{Corresponding very roughly
to 500 photons, but varies with the camera gain.}, the dynamic range of the 
\FD-converter stages. An attempt is made to recover such saturated traces with 
the help of virtual pixel channels in the \FD-camera.

\subsubsection{FdPulseFinderOG}
\label{sssec:reconstruction-pulse-finder}

Next, the exact window in which the \EAS signal lies is determined by a pulse 
finding algorithm. A first approximation of the pulse region is given by the 
sections of the trace that have a \FLT flag (Ref.\ \cref{ssec:fd-triggers}). 
From this initial guess, an iterative process begins to estimate the signal 
window.

Using the \RMS $\sigma_f$, of the uncalibrated trace $f^\mathrm{ADC}$, the 
\SN of the signal window, starting at bin $i$, and ending at bin $j$ is 
calculated. The 1- and 3-bin \SN of the start and stop region are determined as

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pulse-finder-snr}
\SN^{\{i/j\}}_\mathrm{1-bin} = \frac{f^\mathrm{ADC}_{\{i/j\}}}{\sigma_f},
\qquad\SN^{\{i/j\}}_\mathrm{3-bin} = \sum\limits_{n=-1}^{n=1} \frac{f^\mathrm{ADC}_{\{i/j\} + n}}{\sigma_f} 
\qquad\SN = \sum\limits_{n=i}^{j} \frac{f^\mathrm{ADC}_n}{\sigma_f}.
\end{equation}

A new signal window is determined by extending the window by one bin to the
left ($i_\mathrm{new} \rightarrow i-1$) if $s_i > s_j$, or to the right 
($j_\mathrm{new} \rightarrow j+1$) if $s_j > s_i$. The process halts and 
returns the current signal window under the condition that $\SN< 5\,\sigma_f$,
or $\SN^{\{i/f\}}_\mathrm{3-bin} < \sqrt{3}\,\sigma_f$ and 
$\SN^{\{i/f\}}_\mathrm{1-bin} < \sigma_f$. The number of photons $n_\gamma$ 
per \PMT is then calculated as the integral of the calibrated trace from 
$i\rightarrow j$, and has an associated uncertainty of approximately 
\SI{9}{\percent} \cite{abrahamFluorescenceDetectorPierre2010}.

\subsubsection{Arrival Direction Estimator*}
\label{sssec:reconstruction-arrival-direction}

Before the arrival direction of the \EAS is determined, isolated pixels are 
filtered from the \FD-camera image. This is achieved by comparing the pointing 
direction $\vec{P}$ of all pixels with a \SLT pulse within a telescope with 
each other. Pixels that have no pulsed neighbour within a minimum angular 
distance are removed. The remaining pulse pixels are the candidates used in the
subsequent analysis.

Next, the \SDP of the event is determined. The \SDP is the 2D projection of 
the \EAS axis onto the telescope viewing plane (Ref.\ 
\cref{fig:geometry-info}).

It contains a set of arrival direction $(\theta,\,\phi)$, that, without trigger
timing information, look indistinguishable in the \FD-camera. The best fit 
\SDP with normal vector $\vec{n}$ is found by maximizing the cross-products 
$\vec{P}\times\vec{n}$. The orientation of the \SDP is thus fully described by 
the mean pointing direction of all candidate pixels.

Once the \SDP is determined (with an uncertainty of 
$\sigma_{\theta_\mathrm{SDP}} < \SI{0.5}{\degree}$ 
\cite{abrahamFluorescenceDetectorPierre2010}), the orientation of the shower 
axis within the \SDP, $\chi_0$, as well as its minimum separation $R_p$, is 
calculated from trigger timing information.

It follows from geometric considerations that the trigger time $t_i$ of pixel 
$i$ can be expressed in terms of both parameters.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fd-trigger-time}
t_i = t_0 + R_p/c\,\tan\left(\frac{\chi_0 - \chi_i}{2}\right).
\end{equation}

All parameters in \cref{eq:fd-trigger-time} are explained visually in 
\cref{fig:geometry-info}. Determining the \EAS axis thus becomes an exercise of
fitting $R_p$ and $\chi_0$ to the observed pixel trigger times. Whereas earlier
isolated pixels in space have been rejected for the analysis, isolated pixels 
in time, meaning triggers outside of the expected propagation model in 
\cref{eq:fd-trigger-time} are removed as well. On average, the recovered 
arrival direction is accurate to within $\SI{1}{\degree}$ 
\cite{abrahamFluorescenceDetectorPierre2010}.

\subsubsection{Energy Estimator*}
\label{sssec:fd-reconstruction-energy-estimator}

With the reconstructed shower axis at hand, the longitudinal profile of the 
\EAS can be (re)constructed. This is done by propagating the light collected at
the \FD aperture back to the emission point at the shower axis, accounting for 
\UV-attenuation in the air in the process. Doing this for all pixels results in
an estimate of the total luminance of the particle cascade. 

Because the energy estimator relies on the emitted fluorescence light only, 
the different signal components, consisting of direct and scattered 
fluorescence light, as well as direct and scattered Cherenkov light, need to be
separated. This is done by propagating the total luminance at the shower axis 
(calculated in the first step) forward to the telescope. This yields an updated
estimate of $n_\gamma$ where Cherenkov emission and atmospheric scattering are
accounted for. In this fashion, the fluorescence component of the \EAS is 
bootstrapped via iterative propagation and simulation of the observed light 
signal.

The so obtained longitudinal shower profile can be converted into an energy 
loss per grammage by calculating the average energy loss per emitted 
fluorescence photon from hadronic interaction models (Ref.\ 
\cref{fig:fd-profile}). Finally, a Gaiser-Hillas function, using the \USP 
explained in \cite{bellidoShowerProfileFitting2022}, is used to estimate the 
shower maximum, and calculate the energy of the primary particle.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gaiser-hillas-energy}
\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}X} = \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}X}\right\vert_{\Xmax}\,\left( 1 + \frac{R(X-\Xmax)}{L}\right)^{R^{-2}}\,\exp\left(-\frac{X-\Xmax}{RL} \right).
\end{equation}

In \cref{eq:gaiser-hillas-energy} the shape parameters 
$R = \sqrt{\lambda\,/\,|X_0-\Xmax|}$ and $L = \sqrt{\lambda\,|X_0-\Xmax|}$ are
introduced. $\lambda$ and $X_0$ are discussed in 
\cite{hillasCosmicRaysCommonwealth2013}. Once the maximum energy loss, shower
maximum, and $R,\,L$ have been estimated from data, the energy is estimated as
$E_\mathrm{FD} = \xi\,\int\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}X}\,\mathrm{d}X$, where
$\xi>1$ is a correction factor that accounts for invisible energy that is 
radiated away from the shower in the form of neutrinos. The value of $\xi$ is 
derived from Monte Carlo shower simulations 
\cite{barbosaDeterminationCalorimetricEnergy2004}. The per-shower energy 
resolution of the \FD is \SI{10}{\percent} 
\cite{abrahamFluorescenceDetectorPierre2010}.

\subsection{SD Reconstruction}
\label{ssec:sd-reconstruction}

This section details the event reconstruction, which is the recovery of 
high-level physics observables such as the energy of the primary \CR from 
low-level detector measurements like the \WCD \PMT traces and calibration 
histograms.

The reconstruction process is similar for all sub-arrays (Ref.\ 
\cref{ssec:array-structure}) in the surface detector. However, due to the 
grids reaching full efficiency at different energies due to dissimilar detector
spacing, different reference values, quality cuts, and normalization factors 
must be applied during event reconstruction. 

The following sections detail the event reconstruction in the \SD-1500 main 
array, whereas the rest of this work focuses heavily on the \SD-750 infill 
array. Distinctions in the reconstruction process are marked where necessary.

\subsubsection{PMT \& Station Selector*}
\label{sssec:reconstruction-pmt-quality-cuts}

Quality cuts are applied to the \WCD \LPMTs that check several variables 
relating to the functionality of the \PMT. Namely, \PMTs are flagged as bad if
\VemOnline or the standard deviation of the online estimated baseline (Ref.\
\cref{eq:peak-and-charge}) are outside of predefined ranges. The \LG-channel of
the \PMT is flagged as bad if the dynode-to-anode ratio of the \PMT is outside
of a nominal range. Stations for which none of the \LPMTs pass the selection 
criteria receive a "rejected" status, that removes them from participating in 
the event reconstruction.

Next, the dynode-to-anode ratio, the \TTWO timestamp, as well as the \GPS
information of the surviving stations are adjusted and corrected according to 
\LUTs that keep track of station-level software/hardware changes and problems 
that influence any of the reconstructed quantities.

Additionally to the quality cuts on a \PMT level, stations are also checked for
their acquisition time, quality of the \GPS timing information, data integrity,
and many other aspects. Individual stations are flagged as rejected if any of 
the assertions made in the process fail.

\subsubsection{SdHistogramFitterKG}
\label{sssec:reconstruction-histogram-fitter}

Rate-based estimators for \Ivem and \Imip exist on the station-level (Ref.\ 
\cref{sssec:wcd}, \todo{online mip chapter}). During event reconstruction, a
more robust method of estimating the peak and charge of the \LPMTs and the
\SSD-\PMT are used. 

As detailed in \cref{sssec:muon-histograms}, information exists on the 
distribution of pulse heights and integrals for the different \PMTs in the 
\SIrange{1}{2}{\minute} leading up to the event. Crucially, all these 
histograms show a local maximum that can be identified with the most probable 
peak/charge from a single atmospheric muon. The estimated location of the peak 
is derived from the vertex of a second order polynomial that is fitted
to the counts in the histograms.

The found value is not coincident with \Ivem/\Qvem/\Imip/\Qmip, but gives the
most probable peak/charge of an omnidirectional muon. In general, trajectories
with a non-vertical geometry will have a larger track length in the \WCD/\SSD 
compared to \VEMs. Consequently, the detectors record a higher charge for 
omnidirectional events (Ref.\ \cref{fig:ssd-histo}). 

This is corrected for with the help of a constant factor derived from dedicated
measurements using an \SD-station sandwiched by a \RPC hodoscope for track 
selection \cite{assisResultsGianniNavarra2017}. The histogram-based estimate of
the \WCD and \SSD calibration factors is accurate to within 
$\sim\SI{2}{\percent}$ \cite{bertouCalibrationSurfaceArray2006, 
filipCalibrationScintillatorSurface2025}

\subsubsection{SdBaselineFinderKG}
\label{sssec:reconstruction-baseline-finder}

The \PMT baseline estimate (Ref.\ \cref{eq:peak-and-charge}) is equally 
revisited. An algorithm scans the
trace before and after the \EAS signal, and builds a running baseline estimate
that accounts for \PMT undershoot, electronic fluctuations and other hardware 
effects that falsify the physical signal \cite{schulzNewBaselineAlgorithm2023}.
It is crucial to characterize these accurately, as to not bias the calculation 
of trace integrals, as these will influence the estimate of the primary 
particle energy.

\subsubsection{SdTraceCalibratorOG}
\label{sssec:reconstruction-trace-calibrator}

With the knowledge of the bin-by-bin baseline $b_i$, as well as the calibration
constants as determined above, the individual uncalibrated \PMT-traces 
$f^\mathrm{ADC}$ are calibrated according to \cref{eq:offline-calibration}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:offline-calibration}
f^\mathrm{VEM/MIP}_i = \left( f^\mathrm{ADC}_i - b_i \right)\,/\,I_\mathrm{VEM/MIP}.
\end{equation}

Furthermore, the signal window is determined according to the procedure defined
in \cite[Sec. 3.3 of][]{aabReconstructionEventsRecorded2020}. Regions where the
trace exceeds \SI{3}{\ADC} above baseline are identified as signal fragments. 
These signal pieces are iteratively combined in a pairwise fashion under two 
conditions. 

Fragment B must start latest $\SI{500}{\nano\second} + t_A$ after fragment A 
(with duration $t_A$) ends. Additionally, the segments are only merged if the 
charge $Q$ or peak $I$ of both windows fulfill the conditions $Q_A > 0.3\,Q_B$,
or $I_B < 5\,\Ivem$. Both checks ensure the exclusion of random coincidences 
stemming from atmospheric muons.

Once no trace segments are left to merge, the window with the longest duration
is chosen for each \WCD \PMT, and the \EAS start and stop time are calculated 
as the average of the single-\PMT start/stop times. A buffer time of 
\SI{250}{\nano\second} is appended to the stop time, to ensure containment of 
any sub-threshold pulses. 

The integral of the \FADC trace within the determined signal window is 
calculated and divided by the corresponding charge calibration factor. The 
recovered quantity is the total \EAS signal 
$Q^\mathrm{total}_\mathrm{VEM}$/$Q^\mathrm{total}_\mathrm{MIP}$. The 
uncertainty of the total estimated signal is highly non-linear, and increases 
from \SI{5}{\percent} for smaller signals to \SI{60}{\percent} at very large 
signals of $\sim\SI{10}{\kilo\VEM}$ \cite{aabReconstructionEventsRecorded2020}.

\subsubsection{SdSignalRecoveryKLT}
\label{sssec:reconstruction-signal-recovery}

The station-level hardware encodes the \PMT signals using 11-bits. This means 
that signals exceeding $2^{11}-1 = \SI{2047}{\ADC}$ cannot be represented and 
the trace becomes saturated. For the (amplified) \HG-channels, this is observed
for signal amplitudes exceeding very roughly 
$\SI{14}{\Ivem}\,/\,\SI{50}{\Imip}$. The relevant detector information is 
reconstructed from the \LG channel in the case of a \HG-saturated \PMT. For
very high signals ($\sim\SI{440}{\Ivem}\,/\,\sim\SI{1600}{\Imip}$), the \LG
trace is also saturated, and needs to be approximated. In such cases a signal 
recovery algorithm \cite{vebericEstimationSignalSaturated2013} estimates the 
total charge $Q^\mathrm{total}_\mathrm{VEM}$/$Q^\mathrm{total}_\mathrm{MIP}$ by
fitting a Moyal distribution \cite{moyalXXXTheoryIonization1955} to the 
unsaturated portion of the \PMT trace, also accounting for the large \PMT 
undershoot \cite{vebericEstimationSignalSaturated2013}.

\subsubsection{SdEventSelectorOG}

Just before the higher-level reconstruction begins, additional quality cuts are
applied on a station level and event level. Stations that are triggered by 
non-\EAS phenomena (lightning or other \TLEs 
\cite{mussaMultipleELVESOther2025}) are rejected. This module serves mostly to 
remove events that fall into bad periods, where data quality suffers due to 
e.g.\ bad weather, but can also be manipulated to allow for the reconstruction 
of events that do not pass T4 or T5 trigger criteria.

\subsubsection{SdPlaneFitOG}
\label{sssec:reconstruction-plane-fit}

Stations that have not been removed by quality checks up to here are candidate
stations for the \EAS reconstruction. In order to determine the location of the
shower core and the orientation of the shower axis, a weighted sum over all
candidate station gives the event barycenter of station positions and \TTWO 
trigger times.

\begin{equation}
	\vec{r}_\mathrm{bary.} = \frac{1}{\mathrm{W}}\,\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_\mathrm{cand.}} \omega_i\,\vec{r}_i,\qquad t_\mathrm{bary.} = \frac{1}{\mathrm{W}} \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_\mathrm{cand.}} \omega_i\,t^\mathrm{T2}_i,
\end{equation}

where $\omega_i = \sqrt{\Qvem^i}$, and $\mathrm{W}=\sum\,\omega_i$. From this,
a numerical optimizer delivers a first level approximation of the \EAS arrival 
direction by finding the axis that minimizes the difference between the station
trigger times, $t_i^\mathrm{T2}$, and the trigger times expected from a plane 
moving along its' normal direction at the speed of light. 
$\vec{r}_\mathrm{bary.}$ serves as the pivot point of the shower plane axis 
during the optimization process.

To increase the accuracy on the estimated arrival direction, the procedure to 
fit the shower axis to the station trigger times is repeated once more, using a
non-linear parametrization that models the expansion of the \EAS more 
realistically. 

The axis direction $(\theta_\mathrm{rec.},\,\phi_\mathrm{rec.})$ obtained from
this fit is the estimated arrival direction of the \EAS, and is accurate to 
within $\SIrange{1}{2}{\degree}$ \cite{bonifaziAngularResolutionPierre2009}. 
The relative uncertainty on the core position $r_\mathrm{bary.} = (x,\,y)$ is 
given as \SI{2}{\percent} in \cite{ghiaStatisticalSystematicUncertainties2005}.

Station trigger times are hereafter given relative to $t_\mathrm{bary.}$, and 
the \SPD of a station denotes its smallest separation from the shower axis.

\subsubsection{LDFFinderKG}
\label{sssec:reconstruction-ldf-finder}

The next step towards an energy estimate is the parametrization of the \LDF 
(Ref.\ \cref{sssec:particle-footprint}), giving the expected signal, 
$\langle Q_{i} \rangle$ in terms of the \SPD at point $r_i$. Specifically
important for the further event reconstruction is the expected signal 
$S(r_\mathrm{ref.})$ at a shower plane distance $r_\mathrm{ref.}$. This 
parameter is called the shower size.

Intuitively, $\langle Q_i \rangle$ of candidate detector $i$ must decrease with
increasing \SPD, implying that strictly $\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}r}<0$ and
by extension $2\,\beta + \gamma < 0$ (Ref.\ \cref{eq:NKG}).

This constraint on the \LDF slope is used in a numerical fitting algorithm that
finds the parameters $S(r_\mathrm{ref.})$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ via a \NLL 
optimization that tailors \cref{eq:NKG} to $\Qvem$/$\Qmip$ of each candidate 
station.

To boost the accuracy of the recovered fit parameters the optimization is 
executed multiple times, first optimizing the shower size, and gradually 
becoming less constrained, also updating the estimate of $r_\mathrm{bary.}$
(and thus $r$) in the process.

The final estimate of $S(r_\mathrm{ref.})$ has a systematic uncertainty of 
\SI{2}{\percent} \cite{ghiaStatisticalSystematicUncertainties2005}. The main 
array uses $r_\mathrm{ref.} = \SI{1000}{\meter}$, whereas the \SD-750 employs
a smaller reference distance of \SI{450}{\meter} 
\cite{sanchezAMIGADetectorPierre2011}.

\subsubsection{Energy Estimator*}
\label{sssec:sd-reconstruction-energy-estimator}

The shower size obtained in the previous step is a useful metric to estimate 
the primary energy of the \CR particle, as detailed in 
\cite{aabReconstructionEventsRecorded2020}. However, several steps are needed
to recover an accurate estimator $E_\mathrm{SD}$ from $S(r_\mathrm{ref.})$. 

First, a correction is applied to the shower size that accounts for the effect 
of the Earths' geomagnetic field on the shower evolution. Then, another 
correction is applied to $S(r_\mathrm{ref.})$ that aims to eliminate the 
dependence of the shower size on the inclination of the \EAS, i.e.\ arrival 
direction of the primary particle. 

Consider different \EAS of identical energy, but increasing $\theta$. It is 
easy to verify that the track length in the atmosphere grows with increasing 
inclination. As a consequence, horizontal showers are more attenuated, and show
a lower $S(r_\mathrm{ref.})$. 

The attenuation function $f(\theta)$ that describes this phenomenon is 
estimated in an empirical manner by comparing observed distribution of 
$S_\mathrm{ref.}$ for different inclinations to a (without attenuation 
expected) constant reference distribution. 

A third-degree polynomial approximates the attenuation factor sufficiently 
well. From this the zenith independent value $S_{\theta_\mathrm{ref.}}$ is 
calculated via the \CIC. This thus represents the expected shower size that a 
given \CR would have induced if it had an inclination of 
$\theta_\mathrm{ref.}$. It is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cic}
S_{\theta_\mathrm{ref.}} = \frac{S(1000)}{f(\theta_\mathrm{rec.})} = \frac{S(1000)}{ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + 1},\,\mathrm{with}\,x=\cos^2(\theta_\mathrm{rec.})-\cos^2(\theta_\mathrm{ref.}).
\end{equation}

For the \SD-1500 $\theta_\mathrm{ref.} = \SI{38}{\degree}$, and in the \SD-750 
$\theta_\mathrm{ref.} = \SI{35}{\degree}$. The values of $a$, $b$, and $c$ in 
\cref{eq:cic} are given in \cref{tab:cic-parameters}. Finally, 
$S_{\theta_\mathrm{ref.}}$ is used to estimate the primary energy of the \EAS 
as

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:energy-cross-calibration}
E_\mathrm{SD} = A\,S_{38}^B,
\end{equation}

where the power law relation with parameters $A$ and $B$ in 
\cref{eq:energy-cross-calibration} is obtained from a subset of observed \EAS 
that trigger the \FD and the \SD independently. Several quality cuts (Ref.\ 
\cite{aabReconstructionEventsRecorded2020}) ensure that the resulting \SD 
energy scale matches the \FD energy scale and spectrum as closely as possible.
The numerical values of $A$ and $B$ are given in 
\cref{tab:energy-cross-calibration}. The intermediate step of the 
cross-calibration introduces an additional factor in the energy uncertainty 
budget, which grows to $\sigma_{E_\mathrm{SD}}=\SI{14}{\percent}$. 

\subsection{UMD Reconstruction}
\label{ssec:umd-reconstruction}

Observables pertaining to the muon content of \EASs are reconstructed in a 
distinct event reconstruction chain, utilizing data from the \UMD (Ref.\ 
\cref{sssec:umd}).

\subsubsection{MdPatternFinder}

In a first step, the number of muons hitting the \UMD is estimated for every 
bar in every module that passes predefined quality cuts.\footnote{One 
example being if the corresponding \SD-station did not record any trigger.}

Muonic signals are identified by the pattern they leave in the binary trace of
a bar. Namely, runs of 4 or more consecutive ones in the binary trace are 
considered to be caused by a muon. An inhibition window of 12 samples after the
pattern start ensures that large single-muon signals are not overcounted as 
distinct pattern matches. This results in an estimated per-bar number of muons 
$n_{\mu,\,i}$.

An infinite window strategy \cite{hayashidaMuons1GeV1995, 
gesualdiMuonContentAtmospheric2023} is used in which the number of bars with
pattern matches, $k$, out of all $n_b$ bars per module is tied to the binomial 
probability $P(k\,|\,N_\mu)$ of observing $k$ active bars in the module given 
$N_\mu$ impinging muons. The maximum likelihood estimator for $N_\mu$ becomes 
\cite{supanitskyEstimationNumberMuons2021}

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:muon-number}
	\hat{N}_\mu = \left(1 - \frac{k}{n_s}\right)\,/\,\left(1 - \frac{1}{n_s}\right).
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Pileup and Corner Cliping Correction*}

Two effects can skew the value of $\hat{N}_\mu$ away from the true number of 
impinging muons per module. First, the nature of the binary trace makes it 
impossible to recognize two muons entering the same bar at the same time. This
pileup happens especially for $N_\mu \gg 1$ and results in an underestimation 
of the \UMD signal.

The muon count taking pileup into account can be recovered as detailed in
\cite{scornavaccheMuonContentExtensive2025}:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:pileup-correction}
	\hat{N}^\mathrm{PU}_\mu = -n_s\,\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{n_s}\right)\,\hat{N}_\mu.
\end{equation}

Second, there exists a non-zero probability that the trajectory of an inclined muon crosses more than one bar, resulting in a count of $N_\mu\geq2$ stemming 
from a single muon. The corner-clipping probability as determined in 
\cite{jesusStudyMassComposition2025} reads

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:corner-clipping-probability}
	P^\mathrm{CC}(\theta, \Delta\phi) = m(\theta) \left(\left|\sin\Delta\phi\right| - 0.5 \right) + b(\theta), 
\end{equation}

where

\begin{align}
	m(\theta) &= m_0 + m_1\,\left(\sec\theta - 1.2\right), \\
	b(\theta) &= b_1 + b_1\,\left(\sec\theta - 1.2\right),
\end{align}

and $\Delta\phi = \phi_\mathrm{rec} - \phi_\mathrm{MD}$ gives the azimuth
enclosed between the \EAS arrival direction and the \UMD module orientation. 
The corrected muon count becomes

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:corner-clipping-correction}
	\hat{N}^\mathrm{CC}_\mu = \frac{\hat{N}^\mathrm{PU}_\mu}{1 + P^\mathrm{CC}(\theta_\mathrm{rec.}, \Delta\phi)}.
\end{equation}
